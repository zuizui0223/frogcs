# Anonymous cover letter — Journal of Animal Ecology v0.2

Dear Editors,

Please consider our Research Article, **“Recent rainfall predicts broader frog acoustic participation without stronger residual co-calling associations,”** for publication in *Journal of Animal Ecology*.

Environmental pulses can make more species appear in the same observation window for two different reasons: more species may independently become active, or residual associations among species may strengthen. Those mechanisms have different ecological meanings but are easily conflated in community monitoring data.

We examine this distinction using two independent continental frog-acoustic monitoring systems. Recent rainfall was associated with a greater probability of multispecies calling in both standardized North American Amphibian Monitoring Program surveys and expert-validated Australian FrogID recordings. The direction persisted within repeatedly sampled spatial units, and the North American rain coefficient remained negative after temperature and nonlinear seasonal adjustment.

The main contribution is the decomposition of that replicated community signal. In NAAMP, recent rainfall increased the probability that any frog species was calling and increased the size of the acoustically active species pool. In contrast, rainfall did not detectably increase multispecies calling conditional on acoustic activity, pairwise network density, co-calling above an independence expectation derived from species marginal calling frequencies, or mean pairwise excess covariance. We therefore interpret the pattern as shared environmental activation rather than evidence that rainfall strengthens residual interspecific synchrony.

This framing deliberately builds on, rather than competes with, previous work showing rainfall effects on frog calling and chorus onset and with broader community-ecology work distinguishing shared environmental responses from residual species association. We believe the empirical dissociation is relevant to animal ecology beyond frogs: multispecies detections can increase under a shared cue without corresponding evidence for interaction, facilitation or temporal-niche reorganization.

All analyses are observational. The manuscript explicitly distinguishes original versioned endpoints from reviewer-motivated post-opening diagnostics, preserves null secondary results, and reports the small North American primary effect and its borderline complete-route sensitivity. The revised anonymous manuscript is accompanied by reproducible contracts, result receipts and deterministic figure-generation code.
