Please consider our Letter, “Predator timing can reverse the effect of vegetation on offspring in food webs consistent with frog calling patterns,” for publication in *Ecology Letters*.

Can the geography of breeding activity tell us which habitats benefit offspring? In our simulations, food webs consistent with the same broad pattern of frog calling could give opposite answers. When tadpoles shared more of their aquatic season with salamander larvae, shelter from predation could make vegetation beneficial. When their season overlapped chiefly with odonate larvae, vegetation could instead reduce frog abundance by supporting odonate feeding.

The question arose from surveys of 44 frog species at 4,248 North American listening sites. Strong calling declined more steeply with increasing odonate habitat suitability where salamander suitability was higher or flooded vegetation was more extensive. The surveys measured adult breeding activity and landscape context. In stage-structured food webs, we could hold the feeding relationships fixed while shifting the predators’ aquatic seasons. Across two disjoint sets of model structures evaluated at 27 rate combinations, adding both vegetation functions made the observed association signs more frequent even as mean frog abundance fell. In a separate timing experiment, delaying tadpole arrival by 40 days changed a vegetation benefit to a cost under an early salamander calendar and a cost to a benefit under a late one.

Carroll et al. (2023) showed that timing of adult calling can inform overlap among larval frog cohorts. Davenport and Chalcraft (2013) showed that shelter can alter how predators affect tadpoles. Our study connects these questions by identifying when a habitat feature associated with breeding activity can have opposite demographic consequences for offspring. Two further model tests distinguish effects carried into the next season through starting frog density and through reduced odonate recruitment when metamorphosed frogs feed on emerging odonates. For ecologists using breeding distributions to assess habitat value, we predict that the effect of vegetation on offspring should change with the identity of the predator sharing their aquatic season.
