Dear Editors,

Please consider our manuscript, “Food webs that reproduce frog–predator association signs need not benefit frogs,” as a Letter in *Ecology Letters*.

The paper addresses two connected ecological questions. First, does a food web's ability to reproduce characteristic species-association signs imply that it benefits the focal population? Second, can predator developmental conditions reverse the consequences of the same vegetation functions?

The continental observation (C) uses strong adult frog-calling records from 4,248 North American listening sites. Odonate–calling associations depend on caudate suitability and landscape vegetation context, but calling measures breeding-associated behaviour rather than observed tadpole hatching or recruitment. Independent INBO and USGS datasets provide limited supporting associations between recorded calls and detected larvae; these data and their unsuccessful future-year transfer are documented in the Supplementary Information, rather than presented as a third main empirical finding.

Our controlled food-web analyses (D) compare the ability to generate four idealized association signs with the abundance of modelled frog aquatic cohorts. An original screen retained 794/20,000 synthetic proposals; subsequent tests across two fully disjoint samples of 256 food-web structures crossed with 27 hypothetical rate combinations showed average gains in four-sign compatibility (+2.71 and +2.75 percentage points) but declines in modelled frog log-cohort abundance (−0.040 and −0.041). A separately controlled two-factor intervention showed that early salamander arrival together with prolonged aquatic exposure can reverse the net effect of the combined vegetation pathways, from negative to positive within the original model family.

The novelty is a **controlled demonstration that pattern generation and demographic benefit are different ecological outcomes**, plus an explicit developmental condition that can reverse a habitat function's effects. An acoustic correlation is not used as a surrogate for tadpole birth or measured predation. Our model generates idealized sign patterns analogous to field associations but does not identify the actual natural predator network. Supporting observation-link data, sensitivity analyses and unsuccessful geographically held-out field-mechanism predictions remain available in the Supplementary Information.

We believe this distinction will interest readers working on ecological networks, context-dependent trophic interactions, habitat complexity and stage-dependent demographic effects.
