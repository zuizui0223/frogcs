Dear Editors,

Please consider our manuscript, “Food webs that reproduce frog–predator association signs need not benefit frogs,” as a Letter in *Ecology Letters*.

The paper addresses two connected ecological questions. First, does a food web's ability to reproduce characteristic species-association signs imply that it benefits the focal population? Second, can predator developmental conditions reverse the consequences of the same vegetation functions?

The continental observation (C) uses strong adult frog-calling records from 4,248 North American listening sites. Odonate–calling associations depend on caudate suitability and landscape vegetation context, but calling measures breeding-associated behaviour rather than observed tadpole hatching or recruitment. Independent INBO and USGS datasets provide limited supporting associations between recorded calls and detected larvae; these data and their unsuccessful future-year transfer are documented in the Supplementary Information, rather than presented as a third main empirical finding.

Our controlled food-web analyses compare the ability to generate four idealized association signs with simulated frog cohorts. In the model, one vegetation function shelters tadpoles from salamander attack while another supports odonate attacks on tadpoles. At one fixed source setting, the model odonate share of total predation hazard increased from 59.7% to 73.9% when both functions were present. The original screen retained 794/20,000 proposals; two disjoint samples of 256 structures across 27 hypothetical rate combinations showed higher pattern compatibility (+2.71 and +2.75 percentage points) but lower mean frog log-cohort abundance (−0.040 and −0.041). Independently varying salamander arrival and aquatic duration reversed the net vegetation effect under early, prolonged aquatic exposure, as refuge benefits increased and odonate-support costs declined. These are model-conditioned consequences, not measured shifts in wild predation.

The nearest prior work makes this question especially timely. Caldwell et al. (1980) established predation among frog, salamander and dragonfly larvae; Wilbur and Fauth (1990) experimentally quantified multiple-predator effects on tadpoles; Davenport and Chalcraft (2013) showed asymmetric leaf-litter refuge from **adult newts**; and Klecka and Boukal (2014) plus Mocq et al. (2021) showed that habitat structure can facilitate aquatic-invertebrate predators on **invertebrate prey**. Rasmussen et al. (2014) demonstrated size-mediated arrival priority, while Baker et al. (2025) showed multi-channel cascades driven by salamander size variation. We do **not** claim to discover multiple-predator interactions, vegetation's dual roles or priority effects. Rather, the contribution is to combine separately supported pathways and ask an outcome-based question those studies did not directly test: can the **same vegetation intervention** increase the ability to generate a geographically motivated interaction signature while decreasing frog cohorts, and under what predator–prey stage calendars does its demographic sign reverse? The full original-source literature comparison is retained in the author-review provenance ledger.

An acoustic correlation is not a measure of tadpole birth or observed predation, and source-model four-sign acceptance is not a field-calibrated posterior. Our model generates idealized sign patterns analogous to field associations without identifying the actual natural predator network. Supporting observation-link data, compositional sensitivity, exploratory timing calendars and unsuccessful geographically held-out field-mechanism predictions remain in Supplementary Information.

We believe this distinction will interest readers working on ecological networks, context-dependent trophic interactions, habitat complexity and stage-dependent demographic effects.
