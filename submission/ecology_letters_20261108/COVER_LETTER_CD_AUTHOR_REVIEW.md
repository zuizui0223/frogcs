Dear Editors,

Please consider our Letter, “Food webs that reproduce frog–predator association signs need not benefit frogs,” for publication in *Ecology Letters*.

We ask whether food webs that generate a geographically motivated association pattern necessarily benefit the focal population. Across 4,248 North American listening sites, odonate suitability was more negatively associated with adult frog calling in particular caudate and vegetation contexts. Independent Belgian surveys show that ponds with more calling males subsequently yielded more conspecific tadpoles. Whether those ponds also yielded more metamorphs remained uncertain. This opens a demographic question about the weeks that tadpoles spend with different predators.

We independently manipulated two vegetation pathways in a stage-structured model: refuge from salamander predation and support for odonate attacks on tadpoles. Across 27 hypothetical rate combinations and two disjoint samples of 256 food-web structures, the combined functions improved the frequency of four analogous association signs by 2.71 and 2.75 percentage points while decreasing mean simulated frog log-cohort abundance by 0.040 and 0.041. Independently varying salamander arrival and aquatic duration reversed the net vegetation effect in the early-arrival, long-duration setting. These are model-conditioned demographic consequences, not measured wild predation.

The individual ingredients are established, but their implications for these two distinct outcomes have not been jointly tested in the closest studies we identified. Caldwell et al. (1980) and Wilbur & Fauth (1990) documented the relevant predators and tadpole demographic responses; Davenport & Chalcraft (2013) showed asymmetric refuge from **adult newts**; Klecka & Boukal (2014) and Mocq et al. (2021) demonstrated structure-assisted attacks on **invertebrate prey**; and Rasmussen et al. (2014) and Baker et al. (2025) established developmental and multichannel food-web context. Our contribution is therefore not a new trophic link, habitat effect or priority effect. It is the controlled comparison of **pattern generation versus frog demographic benefit**, and the developmental conditions that reverse the latter.

The Supplementary Information retains source-model sensitivity, negative field-model discrimination results, independent larval-detection context and exploratory seasonal tests. A separate source audit documents the close literature and limits of its transferability. We expect the work to interest readers concerned with ecological inference, indirect trophic interactions and life-history-dependent habitat value.
