Dear Editors,

Please consider our Letter, “Food webs that reproduce frog–predator association signs need not benefit frogs,” for publication in *Ecology Letters*.

We ask whether vegetation-mediated food webs that generate characteristic association patterns also benefit the focal population, and when predator developmental timing changes that answer. The empirical starting point is strong adult frog calling at 4,248 North American sites, associated with odonate environmental suitability differently across caudate and landscape-vegetation contexts. Calls index breeding-associated activity, not directly observed tadpole hatching or predation; the INBO and USGS stage observations and their temporal limits are documented in the Supplementary Information.

We separately manipulated two biologically motivated vegetation functions in stage-structured food-web models: refuge for tadpoles from salamanders and support for odonate predation. Across 27 hypothetical rate combinations and two disjoint samples of 256 structures, the combined functions increased the frequency of four analogous interaction signs by **+2.71/+2.75 percentage points**, but reduced mean log aquatic frog cohorts by **−0.040/−0.041**. Independent manipulation of salamander arrival and aquatic duration reversed the net vegetation effect. These contrasts are model-based counterfactuals, not estimates of actual pond attack rates.

The mechanisms themselves have precedents. **Caldwell et al. (1980)** documented tadpole attacks by salamander and dragonfly larvae; **Wilbur and Fauth (1990)** experimentally examined multiple predators in temporary ponds; **Davenport and Chalcraft (2013)** showed asymmetric litter refuge against adult newts; **Klecka and Boukal (2014)** and **Mocq et al. (2021)** showed that structure can facilitate aquatic predators attacking invertebrate prey; **Rasmussen et al. (2014)** identified size-mediated priority effects; and **Baker et al. (2025)** quantified multichannel cascades under salamander size variation. We make no first-discovery claim about these processes. Our distinct contribution is to combine them in a matched intervention test of **association-pattern generation versus demographic benefit**, and to show how stage calendars can reverse the demographic consequence.

The observed acoustic associations motivate rather than validate the simulated trophic mechanism. We retain negative field-prediction tests, species-composition sensitivity, and the limits of observation-stage mapping in the Supplementary Information. A separate primary-literature and novelty audit supports the author-review package.
