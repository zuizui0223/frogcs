Dear Editors,

Please consider our manuscript, “Food webs that reproduce frog–predator association signs need not benefit frogs,” as a **Letter** in *Ecology Letters*.

A common goal of ecological inference is to find mechanisms that reproduce observed species-association patterns. But a good match to an interaction-like pattern need not mean that the inferred process benefits the focal population. We test this distinction using North American frog-calling associations as field motivation and source-audited stage-structured model experiments to separate three outcomes: reproducing an association signature, maintaining frog abundance, and retaining long-term patch occupancy.

Across 4,248 North American listening locations, frog calling showed context-dependent associations with odonate and caudate environmental suitability; these observations do not directly measure aquatic predation. In an original screen of 20,000 synthetic food-web proposals, 794 matched four idealized association directions. A rate- and developmental-context-conditioned null indicated that the 647 selected structures were fewer than the approximately 712 expected by exchangeable acceptance (p=0.0002), although hundreds of candidate models remained. Most importantly, two independent sets of 256 food-web backgrounds, each evaluated across all 27 hypothetical rate combinations, showed average gains in four-sign compatibility (+2.71 and +2.75 percentage points) accompanied by average declines in simulated frog log abundance (−0.040 and −0.041). Independently varying salamander arrival and aquatic-stage duration showed that the demographic effect of vegetation could instead become positive under early arrival and long aquatic exposure.

The simulations are necessary because the available acoustic observations and distribution-model suitability scores do not measure pond-scale predator density, vegetation functions or developmental timing. Their geographical holdout tests did not identify a wild trophic mechanism. Our prespecified virtual-observer sensitivity analysis (R5) serves as a **methodological justification for this modelling approach**: under hypothetical proxy errors, even a known simulated source may be difficult to recover. R5 does not establish why the actual field predictions failed. Thus the study's ecological contributions are the controlled pattern–demography comparison and the developmental reversal of vegetation effects, not a new claim that the natural predator network has been identified.

We believe this distinction between pattern reproduction and ecological benefit, together with the life-history conditions that can reverse benefit, will interest *Ecology Letters* readers studying community assembly, food webs, habitat complexity and demographic consequences of ecological interactions.
