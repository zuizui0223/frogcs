# Aquatic-stage field overlaps for two frog species (2026-11-09)

All evidence here uses dated aquatic-stage detections. The NAAMP acoustic dates and model days were not used to assign larval dates. The primary estimand is a documented opportunity for interaction at a waterbody on a calendar day. These opportunistic sources cannot estimate the relative frequencies of the predator regimes.

## Positive evidence

- **Pseudacris crucifer and larval salamanders.** After manual checking of exact waterbody descriptions in paired vouchers, 12 distinct waterbody-days at 10 localities (not necessarily ten independent populations) are documented in the selected examples. At a Virginia pool, P. crucifer and A. maculatum were collected on both 19 May and 10 June 1979; at a Maryland pond P. crucifer and N. viridescens were collected on 29 May and 11 June 1991. Two detections 22 or 13 days apart do not establish continuous exposure between visits. The broader reviewed list includes caudates outside the five-species field suitability model; use the predator species column for guild-specific claims.
- **P. crucifer and odonate nymphs.** At Brookshire Rd Pond, NC, source site ARMI-104, ten species-identified spring peeper larval samples all bear 28 June 2023 in the USGS file ARMI_DATA.csv. Tornabene et al. report ten Spring Peeper at site BS in the supplement, and 15 larval dragonflies across Aeshnidae, Corduliidae and Libellulidae at BS in Table S6. Coordinates match the USGS site to the tables' precision. The paper states that dragonflies were collected at the same time and sites. This is a study-level same-collection inference anchored to the frog date; Table S6 does not independently timestamp individual dragonflies. [USGS source](https://doi.org/10.5066/P16HVH7D), [published methods](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10646-026-03090-z), [original supplement](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1007%2Fs10646-026-03090-z/MediaObjects/10646_2026_3090_MOESM1_ESM.pdf).
- **Acris gryllus and larval salamanders.** Three waterbody-days in two explicitly identified Florida ponds. Twin Oak Pond: 6 and 9 June 1947, with both Ambystoma talpoideum and Notophthalmus perstriatus. A second, separately described pond: 15 June 1947 with N. viridescens (one of the project's original five pond caudates). Larval stages, species, date and locality matched directly within the same collection.
- **A. gryllus and odonate nymphs.** No verified same-waterbody/date positive pair in this audit. The [Ocala repeated field survey](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/54728.pdf) reports A. gryllus tadpoles on visits including 21 May, 8 June and 12 July 2001 (Table 3), and reports darner and skimmer nymphs in the study wetlands. The available table does not give odonate observations per pond/date. N. perstriatus monitoring also included paedomorphic individuals, so that taxon in the pooled table alone was not classified as confirmed larval exposure. Among the six research-grade iNaturalist larval A. gryllus records checked, five had unobscured locations (two shared one location and day); a same-day 1-km query for annotated Odonata nymphs returned zero. This is a search-coverage check.

## Reproducible GBIF screen

Run `python research/scripts/public_larval_overlap.py CACHE_DIR OUT_DIR` in the repository. US, species keys 2427618/2428182, life stage Larva/Tadpole. Keep PRESENT, nonzero count when given, a single day and a unique GBIF record key. The 236 Mohonk P. crucifer rows labelled Tadpole returned in the GBIF stage query were ABSENT with zero count. Multiple labels may return the same record. The 61 A. gryllus and 394 P. crucifer records represent specimen/occurrence rows, not distinct ponds or animals. Across their 260 dates, retrieve US Caudata Larva and Odonata Larva/Nymph; use exact-day positive records. The published waterbody exemplars are manually reviewed same-dataset, same-state, identical unambiguous locality and day, with preserved larval specimens on both sides. Broad localities, groups of unnamed pools, and coordinate proximity alone were excluded. Duplicate vouchers and multiple caudates were collapsed for waterbody-day counts. The explicit ID list and assertions in the script replay these positives; they do not produce an exhaustive census of overlaps. Each key in the CSV links to https://www.gbif.org/occurrence/ followed by the number; the source dataset registry accompanies the index. GBIF is a mutable index; preserve the cache to recreate the exact query snapshot.

The broader A. gryllus screen returned no Odonata at any stage from 26 distinct georeferenced date/coordinate search boxes (±0.020° latitude, ±0.025° longitude). Many museum records are historical or lack exact coordinates, and herpetological and entomological collections have different sampling effort. These zeros do not indicate absence of nymphs in the ponds. No site-year with both named frog species was identified using exact dataset, state, locality and year among the positive frog vouchers/observations. The available studies are separate from the 317 NAAMP shared listening sites, with no established site linkage.

## Interpretation for the model

The documented overlaps establish that both named frogs can share water with caudate larvae and that P. crucifer can encounter a larval dragonfly guild. None estimates hatch or departure dates, days of joint exposure, relative risk, the sign of vegetation benefit or a natural 78-day tadpole delay. We cannot label a detected salamander visit as a “salamanders only” visit without a comparable odonate survey. A model in which the later-calling A. gryllus always escapes caudates would conflict with its Florida June vouchers; the manuscript's conditional relative-calendar prediction remains open. Comparing the net sign requires repeated stage-resolved sampling of both predator groups and tadpoles at the same waters, with vegetation and demographic outcomes.

Additional scope checks: [Dryad Texas tadpole series](https://doi.org/10.25338/B8G92P) lists A. crepitans instead of A. gryllus; the Louisiana G18 dataset uses A. blanchardi. The [southern Illinois wetland surveys](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-9917/4/2/15) grouped P. crucifer and P. feriarum larvae. None was used as species-specific confirmation for the missing comparison.
