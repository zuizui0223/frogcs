# Do frogs using forest after metamorphosis show stronger woody-wetland moderation?

## Literature-based contrast chosen before estimating NAAMP slopes

A Rhode Island field study monitored adults entering and leaving breeding ponds and emigrating metamorphs across a forest/turf mosaic, and identified the following six NAAMP frog species explicitly in its two contrasting movement/habitat groups. The transferable trait is a categorical difference in nonbreeding forest use and adult movement. The source found many newly metamorphosed individuals more generalist; these groups cannot be interpreted as a quantitative juvenile forest-dependence scale. NLCD woody wetland (class 90, including shrubs) is a landscape variable distinct from upland forest, canopy at a particular pond, or juvenile habitat selection.

| NAAMP name | Evidence used |
|---|---|
| Lithobates sylvaticus (wood frog) | Forest-associated in the Rhode Island comparison. Independent [juvenile movement experiment](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecs2.1202) and [adult terrestrial habitat selection](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/amphibian-terrestrial-habitat-selection-and-movement-patterns-vary-annual-life-history). |
| Pseudacris crucifer (spring peeper) | Forest-associated nonbreeding adults in the [Rhode Island field comparison, pp. 4–5](https://usgatero.lib.msu.edu/v01/n20.pdf). |
| Hyla versicolor (gray treefrog) | Forest-associated nonbreeding adults in Rhode Island, with [independent radiotelemetry and arboreal-refuge study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0006320707003278). The ambiguous H. chrysoscelis/versicolor call label is separate. |
| Lithobates clamitans, L. catesbeianus, L. palustris (green, bull and pickerel frogs) | Three named aquatic-wintering species that readily crossed the woodland/turf edge in the [same field study](https://usgatero.lib.msu.edu/v01/n20.pdf). Green frogs and bullfrogs were relatively tolerant of clearcuts in the [Maine forest experiment](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378112706004919). The comparator definition allows forest use. |
| Lithobates pipiens (additional sensitivity only) | Independently tracked northern leopard frogs in Iowa selected grasslands and wetlands and avoided forest during the tracking season. [Field telemetry](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6030078/). |

We estimated species-specific responses for all 44 named NAAMP frogs where identifiable. The six-species primary panel was restricted to the named groups in the single comparative study. Unclassified taxa are not assigned to the comparator; nine species-complex labels are not distributed among species. taxon_accounting.csv gives coverage. This design does not separate habitat use from phylogenetic history or other correlated traits.

## Analysis

Original 4,248 sites, 432 routes, 63,180 completed visits. Response for each species is the fraction of completed visits with strong calling (index 2–3), including silent visits. This measures reported activity, so local nondetection can reflect range limits or survey timing. We used surveyed-year, visit-weighted 3-km Annual NLCD cover and the primary uniform-background predator SDMs refitted without the flooded-vegetation layer. Joint models use standardized ranks of the species-specific response and both suitability indices, and the same two wetland interaction design as the [pooled analysis](../annual_nlcd_wetlands_20261111/README.md). Wetland cover was either standardized by rank (primary) or centred and expressed in ten-percentage-point units. With caudate rank at its mean, the species contrast D = odonate × woody minus odonate × herbaceous. A negative D indicates that woody wetland shifts the odonate–calling slope more negatively.

The trait test compares the equally weighted mean D of three forest-associated species to the mean D of three comparator species. Each of 1,000 bootstrap samples draws whole routes and preserves correlations among species; ranks are recalculated. Intervals are conditional on this fixed species panel and not corrected for the exploratory species contrasts. The shared-route restriction retains all listening sites on routes where each of the six species had at least one strong call somewhere on the route at some point. It does not require all species to call together. Whole-region and within-route estimates, plus leave-peeper-out, within-Lithobates and additional grassland-comparator sensitivities, are provided. A descriptive enumeration of 20 possible three-versus-three species labels is provided separately; phylogenetically related species are not exchangeable biological replicates.

## Results

On the rank scale, forest-associated species had a more negative woody interaction than comparators (difference −0.055, route interval −0.108 to −0.007). The stronger hypothesis comparing this group difference with the corresponding herbaceous group difference had an estimate of −0.062 [−0.153,+0.023]. Wood frogs and spring peepers had negative D (−0.240 and −0.210); gray treefrogs had positive D (+0.268). The direct group contrast was +0.050 [−0.060,+0.168] within routes. On the 45 shared routes (450 sites) the geographic group difference was +0.123 [−0.135,+0.465]. The equal-area geographic group contrast was negative (−0.234 [−0.414,−0.076]) but attenuated within routes (−0.008 [−0.141,+0.138]). Forest use is a plausible species-level hypothesis; it does not consistently explain why woody rather than herbaceous wetland moderates strong calling. Actual juvenile movement, postlandfall survival and adult recruitment remain unmeasured.

## Reproduction

Run research/scripts/forest_use_wetland_species.py RAW_DIR OUTPUT_DIR with the original hash-verified USGS Counts.csv and Stops.csv (https://doi.org/10.5066/F7G44NG0). species.csv contains all 44 taxa with cohort support and valid bootstrap counts, groups.csv contains panel estimates and direct contrasts, and species_label_check.csv records the small-panel label diagnostic. Undefined ranks or singular fits are left missing.
