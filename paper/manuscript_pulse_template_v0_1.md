# Rainfall gates the re-expression of persistent spatial structure in frog chorus communities

## Abstract

1. Rain is a familiar trigger of frog calling, but knowing that calling increases after rain does not reveal how a spatial breeding assemblage changes. A short environmental pulse could raise activity broadly and independently, or it could switch historically favoured species × sites into a coordinated chorus state. We tested these alternatives using 15 years of standardized North American frog surveys.

2. We analysed 4,236 matched wetter–drier comparisons from fixed ten-stop North American Amphibian Monitoring Program routes. We decomposed changes in calling intensity, spatial depth and the species × site incidence matrix, then compared the observed allocation with a sequence of magnitude-matched null models incorporating dry-state persistence, cross-fitted species-specific rainfall sensitivity, strictly prior site history and a held-out rainfall × history gate.

3. Recent-rain conditions were associated with a larger active footprint, greater local taxonomic depth and greater route richness. More importantly, 65.3% of the rainfall-associated CallingIndex change arose from previously silent species × site cells becoming active, and 87.1% of that activation was direct entry into overlapping or full choruses. Even direct 0→full-chorus transitions increased (β = 0.441, 95% CI 0.114–0.768), including when observer and physical listening-site identity were held constant (β = 0.514, 0.101–0.927).

4. The response was also spatially deep. The unusual excess did not occur when recruited species reached only a second stop, but emerged at third-and-later stops (β = 0.473), where 97.3% of the coefficient was carried by strong chorus states. These wet-state choruses were strongly recurrent in the historical record. Within the same focal pair and species, sites with prior strong chorusing were much more likely to host later wet-state strong chorusing (β = 0.151, 0.129–0.173), and greater rain contrast selectively favoured historically recurrent full-chorus opportunities after normalizing for their availability (β = 0.0245, 0.0070–0.0420).

5. Uniform activation, strong dry persistence, cross-fitted species-specific rainfall responses, strictly prior local history, their joint combination and a held-out rainfall × local-history gate all failed to reproduce the observed taxonomic-versus-spatial allocation. Rainfall-associated frog activity therefore appears not as uniform amplification but as structured re-expression of a persistent chorus landscape. The data identify a fast environmental gate acting on slow species × site structure, while leaving the lower-level biological generator unresolved.

## Introduction — new argument

Rain after dry weather can make a landscape seem to acquire frogs almost instantly. For many anurans this is familiar natural history: reproductive calling is tightly coupled to weather, hydroperiod and season, and some species form intense choruses over short windows. Yet the usual question—whether rainfall increases calling—collapses an important ecological distinction. A louder or richer wet-night assemblage could arise because every local species becomes slightly easier to hear, because species independently respond to the same cue, because particular breeding sites repeatedly switch into activity, or because the community enters a coordinated spatial state.

This distinction matters because frog choruses are both behavioural and spatial. Calling males advertise for mates, interact acoustically with neighbours and use breeding habitats whose suitability can persist or recur across years. Studies of anuran assemblages have shown fine-scale temporal turnover in calling composition, while studies of amphibian and animal site fidelity show that repeated use of locations can be substantial. These literatures establish weather sensitivity, community dynamics and spatial recurrence separately. They do not by themselves show how a short environmental pulse is allocated across a repeated multi-species × multi-site chorus landscape.

We therefore ask whether recent rain simply amplifies an active community or instead gates the re-expression of persistent spatial structure. The distinction can be stated as two timescales. A **fast gate** is the short-term environmental condition that changes whether frogs call. A **slow template** is the recurrent species × site structure indicating where strong chorus activity tends to occur across years. If rainfall acts only as a common activator, then a magnitude-matched increase in cell-level activity should reproduce the observed wet-state matrix. If species differ in rainfall sensitivity, cross-fitted species responses should do so. If repeated breeding-site suitability dominates, strictly prior species × site history should do so. If both matter, their combination—and ultimately a held-out rainfall × history gate—should reproduce the allocation.

The North American Amphibian Monitoring Program provides an unusual test because the same ten-stop routes were repeatedly surveyed under a standardized CallingIndex protocol. We use these data to answer three questions. **First, does rain-associated change mainly amplify existing calling, or switch previously silent species × sites directly into substantial chorus states? Second, when a species enters the wet-state route, does it appear only at isolated sites or deepen across multiple sites, and are those sites historically recurrent? Third, can the observed chorus-state geometry be generated by uniform activation, transferable species sensitivity, prior local history and their combinations?** We use Australian FrogID only for the narrower question of whether wetter conditions also correspond to greater taxonomic depth within already-active acoustic samples; the repeated species × site mechanism is tested only in NAAMP.

This framing turns a familiar response—frogs calling after rain—into a test of how environmental pulses expose the spatial organization of animal communities.

## Discussion — core claims

### Frogs do not simply become uniformly more audible after rain

The full-chorus transition, observer/site controls and multi-site depth make a purely marginal-detection story difficult. However, dry-state zeros remain acoustic nondetections rather than confirmed physical absence, so inference stays at the level of acoustic reproductive activity.

### Rain exposes a persistent chorus landscape

The same species tend to re-enter strong chorus states at physical sites where they have done so before, and the preference for historically recurrent opportunities strengthens with rain contrast. The safest biological interpretation is persistent breeding-site suitability or recurring spatial organization, not individual memory.

### The surprising unit is the species × site chorus state

Species-level rainfall sensitivity alone is not enough. Site history alone is not enough. Their cross-fitted combination is not enough. The community response therefore contains species × place structure that becomes visible under the wet behavioural state.

### A general principle: fast gates can reveal slow ecological templates

Animals can disappear from observation because behaviour switches off without the underlying ecological structure disappearing. Short environmental pulses may therefore reveal persistent spatial organization faster than demographic processes can create it. In frogs, rainfall provides the gate and repeated strong chorusing provides the observable template.

This principle should be presented as a hypothesis generated and strongly supported within NAAMP, not as a universal law.

### What remains unresolved

The data do not distinguish stable hydrology, microhabitat, individual/site fidelity, social facilitation, or other recurring local processes as the lower-level generator. Existing tested proxies for hydroperiod, coarse wetland class, simple social facilitation and multicue weather interactions were insufficient. The result is therefore a community-level ecological pattern with a constrained but unresolved mechanism.
