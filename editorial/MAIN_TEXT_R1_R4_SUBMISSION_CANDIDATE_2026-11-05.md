# Food webs that reproduce frog–predator association signs need not benefit frogs

**Submission main-text candidate · 7 November 2026 · source-backed research draft · not submitted or coauthor-approved**

## Abstract

Across 4,248 North American sites, frog calling varied with odonate suitability depending on caudate suitability. We asked which stage-structured food webs reproduce analogous four-sign patterns and whether their mechanisms benefit frogs. An ABC-style screen retained 794/20,000 proposals (3.97%), representing 647 structures versus 712 under a rate/context-conditioned null; alternative priors yielded 1.09–6.85%. At one exploratory rate, vegetation refuge and predator-feeding support increased compatibility from 3.52% to 14.06%. Across 27 rates, two disjoint 256-structure experiments increased compatibility (+2.71/+2.75 points) while reducing mean log frog abundance (−0.040/−0.041). Of 782 cases where vegetation newly generated the signs, 571 (73%) lost abundance, but this was not an excess loss within matched rates and developmental contexts. Developmental conditions reversed both relative loss patterns and vegetation's demographic effect. Geographically held-out adult-calling predictions did not favor the mechanism over environmental alternatives. Thus, matching interaction-like signs need not identify the underlying field process or predict demographic benefit.

**Keywords:** amphibian calling; caudate guild; dragonfly larvae; conditional associations; ontogeny; vegetation refuge; stage-structured food webs; finite-population persistence

## Introduction

Continental species associations can motivate explanations in terms of biotic interactions, but broad environmental filtering can produce similar geographical patterns. In aquatic food webs, vegetation can both shelter prey and support their predators: leaf litter altered dragonfly predation on tadpoles (Hossie & Murray, 2010), whereas artificial vegetation increased dragonfly attack on planktonic invertebrates (Mocq et al., 2021). These examples suggest opposed habitat functions without demonstrating vegetation-assisted dragonfly predation on frogs. We ask which food webs reproduce idealized directions of geographical frog–predator associations, and whether pattern-generating processes also benefit frogs.

Predator development provides a candidate source of this reversal. Arrival-order manipulations in amphibian communities (Alford & Wilbur, 1985) and size-mediated priority effects among dragonflies (Rasmussen et al., 2014) demonstrate the importance of developmental context. Dragonfly risk also altered larval salamander traits (Kishida et al., 2009), while dragonfly-induced tadpole refuge use reduced adult-newt foraging (Davenport & Chalcraft, 2013). We therefore examine predator arrival and aquatic-stage duration as separate determinants of vegetation effects in a modelled frog–odonate–salamander food web. Separating these inputs is necessary when a developmental treatment changes both.

Regional surveys can reveal conditional associations that motivate these mechanistic questions. However, spatial co-occurrence alone does not establish ecological interactions (Blanchet, Cazelles & Gravel, 2020). A geographical association may reflect trophic processes, shared environmental filtering or observation processes. Pattern-oriented modelling uses multiple patterns to constrain candidate mechanisms (Grimm & Railsback, 2012); demographic consequences provide a further outcome to evaluate. A mechanism's ability to generate an interaction-like signature need not imply a benefit to the focal population.

We use repeated North American acoustic surveys to describe how odonate-suitability–frog-calling associations change across caudate environments. Calling measures breeding-associated activity rather than tadpole abundance or recruitment, and species-distribution-model (SDM) suitability represents occurrence–environment associations rather than predator density. We then test possible generators of idealized interaction directions and quantify their population consequences. The core comparison is between a model's ability to reproduce association *directions* and its demographic consequences; independently varying predator arrival and aquatic duration further tests why those consequences can reverse.

**R1** describes geographical acoustic associations and their alternatives, with fixed model-derived predictions (G4/G5), retrospective context diagnostics (G6) and a species-group comparison (G7). **R2/G12** screen food webs over synthetic rates and audit prior dependence before the exploratory-rate vegetation factorial. **G8–G11** test all 27 synthetic rates in separate, nonoverlapping 256-structure samples without selecting successes. **R3** measures predator-specific hazards and frog abundance. **P1** independently crosses salamander arrival and aquatic duration to resolve the demographic reversal. **R4** follows the same 256 backgrounds into a stochastic 80-year population model. These layers distinguish observed associations, model-generated directions and demographic effects.

## Materials and methods

### R1. Observed acoustic association and alternative explanations

We used North American Amphibian Monitoring Program (NAAMP) records from **4,248 repeatedly surveyed listening locations**, comprising **63,180 completed surveys during 2001–2015** across 432 route components (Foreman et al., 2017). Frog acoustic response was the fraction of completed visits on which at least one frog species was recorded at the strong-calling threshold. We constructed potential guild environment predictors from 28 eligible SDM-modelled taxa: 15 frogs, seven caudates and six odonates. The main odonate predictor (**O**) was mean environmental suitability for the odonate guild; **S** represented mean suitability for pond-breeding caudates. Species suitability surfaces fitted with uniform and target-group backgrounds were projected to the same monitoring locations. Background choice was a sensitivity analysis, not independent field replication (Phillips et al., 2009).

We estimated Spearman correlations between O and the strong-calling response across low, middle and high S-suitability tertiles, comparing the highest and lowest groups. Original 1,000-draw route-cluster bootstrap intervals accounted for route clustering. A landscape vegetation proxy (**V**) indicated whether flooded vegetation was recorded within 3 km of each acoustic monitoring site (Tuanmu & Jetz, 2014). This proxy is not a pond-level inventory of submerged stems or refuge. We retained whole-region and within-route comparisons, including interaction terms for O×S, O×V, S×V and O×S×V.

To examine whether an observed conditional association carries independent predictive information, we preserved five spatially blocked folds based on **56 two-degree route-centroid blocks** (Roberts et al., 2017). Predefined ridge-binomial acoustic regressions included survey, climate, water, land cover and geographic controls, with or without O×S. More flexible alternatives added 21 predeclared nonlinear combinations of six environmental variables. All model scores were evaluated on spatially held-out locations, with geographic block-bootstrap uncertainty; no held-out acoustic outcome was used to select predictor sets.

We further distinguished the *recording* of any frog call from the *strength* of a recorded call. The original visits included **47,912 any-call** and **36,554 strong-call** events, with 66 sites lacking any detected call. A two-part statistical observation model separately predicted P(any call) and P(strong call | any call); their product predicted the unconditional strong-call probability. We compared this prediction with a direct strong-call model on the same spatial heldouts and fixed predictor designs. This procedure changes the representation of adult acoustic observations only; it is neither a calibrated detection/occupancy model nor a larval-to-adult demographic link. Full response-definition and spatial sensitivity methods are retained in Supplementary Methods.

We also prespecified a vegetation×species-group comparison contrasting tiger-salamander suitability with the other Ambystoma species, retaining newts separately and the original spatial blocks. Overlapping larval durations prevent interpreting this contrast as measured developmental timing (G7; Supplementary Methods S3e).

### R2. Source-defined trophic mechanisms and independent vegetation-function manipulation

The original mechanistic space comprised **3,600 canonical stage-structured food-web models** encoding direct and indirect trophic links, vegetation functions, developmental timing and feeding responses. A genuine seasonal predator–prey model was embedded in an **80-year delayed-recruitment model**. For each model/parameter proposal, we evaluated eight source conditions formed by two vegetation levels and low/high caudate and odonate supply. The response was the mean aquatic frog cohort over the final 20 years. The original model-native four-sign criterion required **OS−, OV−, SV+ and OSV+** on the log aquatic frog response, using the frozen \(10^{-6}\) sign threshold. The rejection ABC-style screen targets idealized model signs, **not a field-conditioned posterior for NAAMP calls**.

We reaggregated all **20,000 originally simulated structure–development–rate proposals**, reporting proposal denominators and signs in the complete 3×3×3 grid of synthetic attack coefficient (0.5, 1.5, 3), local recruitment fraction (0.25, 0.50, 0.75) and maturation conversion (2, 4, 8). An artificial 5% survival floor was retained. G12 reweighted all proposals under nine illustrative synthetic rate priors and varied sign thresholds and response scale (Supplementary Methods S14). The historically selected low-attack/high-recruitment region is exploratory, not field-calibrated.

To test the structural hypothesis without comparing different models that contain different hidden features, we next **froze a new sampling seed before inspecting any new result** and sampled **256 distinct legal original-catalogue backgrounds** without replacement. These backgrounds were chosen to permit the two specific vegetation features to be toggled while leaving all other model flags intact. Original hypothetical rates were held constant within each quartet, with attack=0.5 and local recruitment fraction=0.75 retained from the historical exploratory regime. Two features were crossed: **frog refuge from salamander attack** (\`cover_SF\`) and **vegetation-supported odonate attack on frogs** (\`support_DF\`). The four legal original-catalogue states were 00 (neither), 10 (frog refuge only), 01 (odonate feeding support only) and 11 (both). All three original developmental contexts were evaluated, yielding **3,072 genuine 80-year model proposals** and 24,576 vegetation×caudate×odonate source responses.

The prespecified contrasts were both-versus-neither compatibility (\(p_{11}-p_{00}\)) and nonadditive factorial compatibility (\(p_{11}-p_{10}-p_{01}+p_{00}\)). We used 10,000 bootstrap draws at the **256-background** unit; developmental contexts and four intervention arms remained paired. The auxiliary 5% minimum-survival result was reported alongside, not used to select favorable source backgrounds.

For field–model comparisons (G4/G5), we checked support for low/high O×S×V conditions and averaged source log cohorts equally across all 256 backgrounds and three developmental contexts, including failures. Each plant state's eight-corner response was decomposed into polynomial terms; its OS, OV, SV and OSV terms formed one fixed predictor. This isolates a source interaction component without statistically orthogonalizing it against observed environmental covariates. A nonnegative logit coefficient fitted within each training fold imposed an uncalibrated monotonic larval-to-calling link. Fixed 11/00 shapes and environmental alternatives shared the same spatial holdouts (Supplementary Methods S3c).

After examining the pooled G5 results, G6 retrospectively decomposed those saved predictions by recorded flooded vegetation (506 V=1; 3,742 V=0 sites), without refitting or changing folds, thresholds or source models. We compared NLL per visit and the R1 high-minus-low S correlation contrast for observed k/n and predicted calling probability. Paired 56-block resampling (999 draws) quantified conditional uncertainty; original R1 route-bootstrap intervals were retained. Comparing smooth probabilities with noisy k/n is a descriptive response-shape diagnostic, not independent field validation. Intervals are pointwise and conditional on the fixed predictions.

### G8–G11. Same-rate grid and disjoint structural replication

We extended the original paired vegetation-function factorial without retuning the biological kernel or selecting successful model structures. All 256 previously sampled legal backgrounds, three original developmental contexts and all four vegetation arms (00/10/01/11) were crossed with **27 fixed hypothetical rate cells**: attack coefficients 0.5/1.5/3, local recruitment fractions 0.25/0.50/0.75 and maturation conversion factors 2/4/8. This yielded **82,944 genuine 80-year model scenarios and 663,552 environmental vertices**. We compared paired four-sign acceptance, artificial 5%-survival compatibility and log frog cohorts across all sources. Ten thousand matched-background resamples yielded model-conditional 95% intervals. G8 and its disjoint G11 replication, both independently source-audited across 64 shards, are documented in Supplementary Methods S10 and S13.

### R3. Directly auditable model predator hazards and frog cohorts

For the same 256 backgrounds, we retained the original seasonal integrated per-frog hazards from salamander attack (**S→F**) and odonate attack (**D→F**) under vegetation-present/high-S/high-D source conditions. We calculated the model odonate share separately within each source as \(H_{DF}/(H_{SF}+H_{DF})\), conditional on the denominator being defined, then summarized source-level shares. Importantly, the **mean of source-specific hazard shares is not the ratio of the two separately averaged hazards**. These hazards measure modelled per-larva attack pressure, not numbers of real tadpoles eaten. All vegetation-function arms had to produce identical biological source outcomes when vegetation was absent, a preregistered numerical negative control.

We also compared the same matched model backgrounds for mean aquatic frog log cohorts and the conditional four-sign success indicator. The two outcomes use different scales: a higher compatibility rate need not correspond to a larger frog population.

### P1. Separating arrival order from aquatic-stage duration

In the original three-context experiment, the early-salamander setting altered both salamander arrival and its aquatic duration. We therefore froze a **new 2×2 developmental intervention** before calculating outcomes, retaining the same original 256 legal model backgrounds, attack and recruitment values, and all four vegetation treatments. Frog arrival (day 20), frog aquatic duration (45 days), odonate arrival (day 20) and odonate aquatic duration (60 days) were held constant. Salamander arrival was varied independently (early day 0 versus late day 40) of salamander aquatic duration (short 40 versus long 60 days). This generated **4,096 genuine 80-year model scenarios** and 32,768 original vegetation×salamander×odonate source responses. Late/short and early/long reproduced the prior original contexts 1 and 2 exactly across 2,048 source scenarios and 16,384 original eight-corner responses.

Within each background, we computed the both-functions-minus-neither difference in mean log aquatic frog abundance and four-sign compatibility under all four developmental settings. The pure arrival and pure duration moderators were averages across the opposite factor; their interaction was the usual difference-in-differences. All original 256 backgrounds remained in the analysis; **10,000 source-background bootstrap resamples** retained all timing and treatment comparisons paired. This separates two *model inputs*, not actual pond breeding chronology. A separate response-blind GBIF egg-stage eligibility audit did not meet the preregistered taxon-coverage threshold for a field breeding-lead analysis, which was therefore not performed.

After completing P1, we decomposed its net two-function effect into refuge-only, feeding-support-only and nonadditive components. All 256 backgrounds and four timings were retained, with shared source-bootstrap indices. This post-hoc explanation also counted background-level sign reversals (Supplementary Methods S8b).

### R4. Same-background stochastic long-term population consequences

We carried *all* 256 newly sampled food-web backgrounds—not only the four-sign successes—into the unchanged stochastic finite-population version of the original annual model. The design crossed three developmental contexts, four vegetation interventions, two hypothetical count-per-density scales (**q=3,12**), movement OFF/ON and two stochastic process seeds, producing **24,576 genuine 80-year model arms** on an eight-patch artificial ring. The model retained delayed recruitment and **external immigration ON**. Hence a local 0→positive patch transition cannot be attributed uniquely to movement. PR160's original population kernel and all biological rates were retained.

Primary responses were year-80 occupied frog patches (0–8) and the matched vegetation factorial on this endpoint, examined separately for q, movement and developmental context. Secondary outcomes were years61–80 mean patch occupancy, mean frog count, local extinctions and recolonizations, and the difference of movement effects between plant states. Original stochastic streams were keyed partly by plant-arm draw IDs; equal biological rates and process seed labels do not imply identical random-number trajectories. We averaged the two process seeds within each source/background and estimated conditional Monte Carlo intervals using 10,000 resamples of the 256 original backgrounds. These intervals characterize synthetic model-source variation, **not confidence intervals on wild trophic or demographic parameters**.

## Results

### R1. Third-guild context is observable but does not identify trophic control

The geographical odonate-suitability–frog strong-calling association was weak in aggregate but heterogeneous across pond-caudate-suitability environments. The high-minus-low S-suitability Spearman correlation contrast was **−0.385** (route-bootstrap interval −0.509 to −0.240) under uniform SDMs and **−0.179** (−0.328 to −0.029) under target-group SDMs. Within routes, the corresponding contrasts were **−0.122** (−0.199 to −0.034) and **−0.072** (−0.183 to +0.023). Thus the uniform-background within-route pattern remained negative, whereas the target-group within-route interval included zero.

The O×S predictor did not yield a consistent spatially held-out improvement once more flexible shared-environment terms were included. For the direct strong-calling response, the incremental negative-log-likelihood gain per completed visit was approximately **−0.00009** under each background, both with 56-block intervals spanning zero. The observed regional contrast therefore cannot be interpreted as independent evidence of realised odonate or caudate larval predation.

The acoustic definition also mattered. Under uniform SDMs, the within-route high-minus-low contrast for strong calling was more negative than the corresponding contrast for *any* recorded calling: **−0.122 versus −0.057**, with their paired difference **−0.064** (−0.116 to −0.012). This difference was not supported under target-group SDMs. A two-part any-call × strong-given-any observation model modestly improved spatial prediction over a single direct strong-call model under additive environmental predictors (negative-log-likelihood gain per visit **+0.000415** uniform and **+0.000860** target group; both block intervals positive). Yet adding O×S to the flexible conditional strong-given-any model did not improve prediction in either background, and worsened it under target-group SDMs. The two-part diagnostic identifies acoustic *response-definition sensitivity*, not a calibrated biological connection to tadpoles.

We constrained adult calling predictions using one fixed interaction pattern averaged across all 256 model backgrounds. The nonnegative 11 coefficient was positive in all five training folds under both backgrounds; the 00 coefficient was zero in all folds. The source-11 shape gave small positive held-out NLL point gains over a nonlinear environmental baseline under both SDM backgrounds (+0.000699 uniform; +0.000232 target-group), whereas the source-00 alternative contributed approximately zero. However, both 56-block intervals spanned zero, a simpler additive environmental model still predicted better, and one of 16 field eight-corner comparison cells failed the prespecified site-support gate. The model-derived pattern therefore made a small, uncertain contribution to held-out calling predictions; the simpler environmental model remained more accurate (Supplementary Methods S3c).

The retrospective G6 diagnosis localized the positive point gains to the 506 V=1 locations: **+0.00627** (56-block 95% interval −0.002351 to +0.013237) uniform and **+0.00309** (−0.005462 to +0.009389) target-group NLL per visit, relative to the nonlinear environmental baseline. At 3,742 V=0 locations, gains were **−0.000024** and **−0.000139**, respectively. Both paired V=1-minus-V=0 intervals included zero. Observed and source-11 predicted high-minus-low S correlation contrasts were also less negative at V=1 under both backgrounds (Supplementary Table S3e). However, the simple additive environmental model had lower NLL and also generated the pooled negative rank contrast. This locates directional agreement in a relevant habitat context; these comparisons **do not establish** a distinct natural predator mechanism or a causal vegetation effect.

The G7 vegetation×species-group coefficient was positive but uncertain: **+0.067 [−0.142,+0.319]** under uniform SDMs and **+0.028 [−0.214,+0.267]** under target-group SDMs. Adding it produced negative held-out NLL point gains (**−0.000196/−0.001100**), with intervals spanning zero. Thus this comparison did not support the proposed positive moderation (Supplementary Methods S3e).

G14's **794-model ABC-accepted template** improved heldout adult-calling NLL over flexible additive environment under uniform SDMs (**+0.001155** [0.000253,+0.002427]) but not robustly under target-group SDMs (**+0.000163** [−0.000548,+0.000931]). G15 then compared it with a fixed sign-only shape and **199 rate/context-preserving source-null templates**. ABC beat sign-only in both backgrounds, but was better than **199/199 nulls under uniform and 0/199 under target-group**. Neither G14 nor G15 passed its dual-background gate. G16 localized **95.6%** of background discrepancy to landscape-V0 sites. After G17's fixed numerical replay failure, a separate post-hoc G17b cross-swap attributed reversal chiefly to **source O/S suitability ranks**, not additive environment (Supplementary Methods S16–S19).

### R2. The generator depends on recruitment, predation and combinations of vegetation functions

Of the 20,000 original 80-year model proposals, **794 (3.97%)** generated all four idealized interaction directions. Compatibility was higher at attack=0.5 (**421/6,555; 6.42%**) than attack=3 (**146/6,696; 2.18%**) and at recruitment=0.75 (**424/6,689; 6.34%**) than 0.25 (**106/6,607; 1.60%**). The weak-attack/high-recruitment region yielded **179/2,245 (7.97%)** versus **11/2,228 (0.49%)** for strong attack/low recruitment; the highest individual rate cell had *intermediate* attack (**78/710; 10.99%**). An artificial 5% survival condition reduced accepted proposals from 794 to **426**, with **none of 146** four-sign successes under attack=3 passing it. The 794 accepted proposals spanned **647 canonical IDs** (G13 rate/context-conditioned null: **712**, p=0.0002); G12 rate priors shifted acceptance to **1.09–6.85%**, while log-sign epsilon=0.01 retained **267**.

Within the historical low-attack/high-recruitment region, source models combining \`cover_SF\` and \`support_DF\` generated the four signs in **106/675 (15.70%)** proposals, compared with **12/424 (2.83%)** when both flags were absent. Across the full prior, G13 found **support_DF=1** enriched by **17.47 points** after rate/context adjustment (familywise p=0.0002); these cross-model associations still cannot identify a wild causal mechanism.

The prospective 256-background same-model intervention resolved that ambiguity at the model level. Across 256 structures and three original developmental contexts, compatibility was **27/768 (3.52%)** with neither function, **44/768 (5.73%)** with only frog shelter, **60/768 (7.81%)** with only odonate feeding support, and **108/768 (14.06%)** with both. The matched both-minus-neither increase was **+10.55 percentage points** (source-background Monte Carlo interval +7.68 to +13.67), while the **nonadditive factorial contrast was +4.04 points** (+0.91 to +7.29). These contrasts also remained positive under the source's artificial 5% survival sensitivity. However, the nonadditive effect differed by developmental setting: it was +7.42 and +7.81 points in contexts 0 and 1, but **−3.13 points** in the early-salamander context 2, where the negative interval did not remain distinct from zero after adding the 5% survival gate. Vegetation-function synergy was therefore not universal across source life histories.

### G8. Rate sweeps reproduce a pattern–population mismatch; G11 repeats it

The complete 27-rate paired experiment increased four-sign compatibility from **2.252%** with both vegetation functions OFF to **4.958%** with both ON: a mean increase of **+2.705 percentage points** (source-bootstrap interval **+2.021 to +3.410**), equally weighted over all rates and three developmental contexts. The increase remained **+2.619 points** (+2.054 to +3.226) when compatibility also required the artificial 5% survival floor. Nevertheless, the same interventions **reduced mean log aquatic frog cohorts by −0.04030** (−0.04918 to −0.03109). The pooled factorial nonadditivity was **−0.709 points** (−1.461 to +0.010), showing that the positive interaction at the historically selected rate regime was not general across rates.

Across rates, compatibility improved in **15/27** cells, declined in seven and was unchanged in five; nine and three pointwise intervals were respectively positive and negative (unadjusted). All nine weak-attack cells improved (mean **+7.827 points**), versus +0.521 at attack 1.5 and −0.231 at attack 3. Mean frog-cohort effects were negative in **all 27 cells** when contexts were pooled.

The developmental context altered that conclusion for abundance. Pooling equally across all 27 rate cells, the original contexts 0 and 1 had mean log frog-cohort effects of **−0.07227** (−0.08327 to −0.06103) and **−0.07384** (−0.08525 to −0.06217); original context 2 instead had **+0.02521** (+0.01507 to +0.03543). Context 2 was positive in 20/27 cells; these original contexts jointly altered developmental features. P1 independently separated arrival and duration at its fixed rate (Supplementary Table S10a; Figure S4).

G9 counted **571/782 (73.0%)** newly sign-generating cases with lower frog cohorts; G10 found no clear rate×context-adjusted excess loss (**−4.93 points [−11.28,+0.83]**; S11–S12). In G11, **256 previously unused legal structures**, crossed with the same 27 rates, reproduced both pre-fixed directions: compatibility **+2.754 points [2.083,3.434]**, mean log frog cohorts **−0.04125 [−0.05018,−0.03202]**. Among **835** new-sign cases, **612 (73.3%)** lost cohorts; developmental context2 again showed a positive mean abundance effect (S13).

### R3. Predator-hazard redistribution does not guarantee greater frog abundance

The original model's seasonal hazards responded in opposite directions to the two vegetation manipulations. Under the vegetation-present/high-S/high-D source corner, mean S→F hazard declined from **0.624 to 0.376** when both functions were present, while mean D→F hazard rose from **0.488 to 0.945**. The *mean of separately calculated source-level* D→F hazard shares increased from **59.7% to 73.9%** (paired source-background difference +14.22 percentage points, interval +12.90 to +15.61). At vegetation=0, the four treatments gave identical original model states and hazards, satisfying the numerical negative control.

Despite generating the four-sign pattern more frequently, the combined plant functions **reduced mean log aquatic frog cohorts** relative to neither function by **−0.0841** (source-background interval −0.1128 to −0.0557). Frog refuge alone increased that mean, while odonate feeding support alone reduced it. The combined net effect was negative in developmental contexts 0 and 1 but positive in context 2. A vegetation function that improves the likelihood of an interaction *signature* thus need not benefit the focal frog cohort. The hazard-direction effect is partly built into the model's mechanism definitions; its joint consequences for interaction geometry and frog abundance are the tested simulation outcomes.

### P1. Independent timing and duration manipulations reverse demographic benefit as signature gains diminish

The controlled developmental factorial resolved the previously confounded early-salamander result. Relative to neither vegetation function, both functions changed mean log aquatic frog abundance by **−0.1864** (source-bootstrap 95% interval −0.2266 to −0.1464) under late arrival/short duration, **−0.1860** (−0.2271 to −0.1463) under late arrival/long duration, **+0.0021** (−0.0264 to +0.0317) under early arrival/short duration and **+0.1163** (+0.0787 to +0.1560) under early arrival/long duration. Thus earlier salamander arrival alone largely eliminated the negative vegetation effect, but **a reliably positive abundance effect required both early arrival and prolonged aquatic exposure** within this model. Across the two duration levels, early versus late arrival increased the vegetation effect on mean log F by **+0.2454** (+0.1967 to +0.2976). Longer versus shorter duration increased it by **+0.0574** (+0.0438 to +0.0714), with a positive arrival×duration interaction **+0.1138** (+0.0880 to +0.1409).

Yet vegetation's increase in four-sign compatibility was greatest when the effect on frog abundance was negative. Both-functions-minus-neither compatibility increments were **+13.28, +10.94, +9.38 and +2.34 percentage points** across late/short, late/long, early/short and early/long settings, respectively. Advancing arrival reduced this compatibility advantage by an average **6.25 points** (−10.74 to −1.95), and lengthening duration reduced it by **4.69 points** (−7.23 to −2.15). The model condition that most strongly benefited frog abundance therefore provided the **smallest incremental gain in producing the idealized signature**. This further separates compatibility from demographic benefit, while leaving actual natural breeding priority unverified.

The post-hoc decomposition attributed the late/short-to-early/long shift to increased refuge benefits (**+0.1719**, interval +0.1422 to +0.2042) and reduced feeding-support costs (**+0.1476**, +0.1157 to +0.1829), partly offset by nonadditivity (**−0.0167**, −0.0248 to −0.0093). Together these produced a **+0.3028** shift in the net vegetation effect. Of 256 backgrounds, **124 changed from negative to positive**, none changed in reverse, and 73 remained negative. Thus the mean reversal represents a broad, heterogeneous response within the sampled model family (Supplementary Methods S8b).

### R4. Long-term patch persistence is stage-dependent and can diverge from both signatures and abundance

The same new 256-model backgrounds produced **24,576 genuine stochastic 80-year finite-population model arms**. Yet the strong R2 improvement in sign generation did **not** consistently translate into a net increase in occupied frog patches. The pooled year-80 both-minus-neither contrast was **−0.022** (−0.101 to +0.056) occupied patches at q=3 with movement OFF, and **+0.029** (−0.037 to +0.096) with movement ON. Under q=12, effects were near zero because approximately **96–98%** of the original source arms occupied all eight artificial patches at year 80. The factorial interaction on year-80 occupancy was nevertheless **+0.100** (+0.018 to +0.180) patches for q=3 with movement ON, while the *net* both-minus-neither gain still included zero.

Developmental context changed the direction of the long-run outcome. At q=3 with movement ON, both plant functions versus neither altered year-80 frog patch occupancy by **−0.021** (−0.107 to +0.070), **−0.086** (−0.176 to +0.006) and **+0.195** (+0.037 to +0.354) patches across contexts 0, 1 and 2. These original contexts vary salamander arrival, salamander aquatic duration and odonate aquatic duration; R4 therefore does not isolate arrival order as P1 does.

Meanwhile, the combined functions **reduced years61–80 modelled frog count**, averaged across contexts: by **−5.32 individuals** (−7.20 to −3.54) at q=3 with movement and **−23.65** (−30.81 to −16.92) at q=12 with movement, summed across the artificial ring. Local recolonizations and extinctions could increase together, rather than signalling greater persistence. Movement itself raised model occupancy at low count scale, but the *additional movement advantage* associated with both vegetation functions remained uncertain: q=3 **+0.051** (−0.042 to +0.147), q=12 **+0.014** (−0.001 to +0.029) patches. Because external immigration remained on, these findings cannot identify movement as the sole cause of recolonization.

In context 2 at q=3, occupancy also increased with movement OFF (**+0.197**, +0.047 to +0.344). Frog-count gains were **+2.12** (+0.59 to +3.63) without movement and **+1.91** (+0.36 to +3.46) with movement.

## Discussion

### Reproducing an association sign need not benefit the focal population

G13 confirmed **structural selectivity beyond synthetic rate/context alone** (647 accepted IDs versus 712 expected, p=0.0002), yet hundreds of models remain compatible; this is **not a field posterior**. Disjoint G8/G11 source samples both gained mean compatibility across 27 rates while reducing mean frog cohorts. G9 and G10 show that newly sign-generating cases frequently lost frogs, but were not more loss-prone overall after rate×context standardization; developmental contexts showed opposing associations. Positive compatibility gains were concentrated in weak-attack regimes and could reverse at stronger attack; the original positive nonadditive signature effect did not persist over all rates. Thus matching a pattern's direction neither uniquely selects a pathway nor reliably predicts demographic benefit. These model contrasts are not quantitative fits to adult calling.

### Predator development determines when vegetation benefits prey

The controlled timing factorial identifies a positive demographic outcome and the combination of developmental conditions that produces it. Earlier salamander arrival largely removes the negative vegetation effect, while prolonged aquatic presence after early arrival makes the effect positive. The positive arrival×duration interaction shows that these inputs act jointly. Within this model family, predator development therefore changes whether the combination of frog shelter and odonate feeding support benefits the frog cohort. This result gives a specific mechanistic prediction beyond a general expectation that habitat effects vary with context.

At the previously selected synthetic attack=0.5 and local-recruitment=0.75 setting, the two vegetation functions jointly increased four-sign compatibility across an independent sample of 256 model backgrounds. Positive nonadditivity in two developmental contexts and antagonistic nonadditivity in the third show that their effects depend on life history; G8 shows that positive nonadditivity is not a rate-general feature. The opposing predator-hazard changes follow the encoded pathways; the demographic reversal and interaction-pattern outcomes are the tested consequences. Pond mesocosms likewise show that habitat-complexity effects depend on predator identity and intraguild context (Anderson & Semlitsch, 2016). The conditional R4 occupancy benefit extends the range of possible demographic benefits, although the pooled effect was uncertain and the larger count scale approached saturation. In P1, the developmental setting with the greatest abundance benefit had the smallest compatibility gain, so pattern fit alone would not identify the most favourable demographic setting.

These simulation findings are conditional in three ways. First, the attack and local recruitment region was selected after examining a larger synthetic model prior and **was never inferred as the true biological rate range** from the acoustic data. Second, a common sign pattern can be generated by distinct food-web structures, and successful models are not a field-conditioned ABC posterior. Third, the stochastic long-run ring experiment uses artificial patch geometry, limited count scales, an external immigrant source and model-defined developmental contexts. Positive numerical counterfactuals within this model family demonstrate **possible consequences of the stipulated mechanisms**, not their realized magnitudes in monitored ponds.

### Third-guild context is an observation, not a predator census

Shared environmental filtering still plausibly explains adult calling. G5/G7 did not establish a wild trophic mechanism, and G14/G15 gains were uniform-SDM-specific. G16/G17b traced this sensitivity to landscape-V0 sites and O/S ranks; G17's original replay gate failed. In independent USGS surveys, calls and tadpoles co-occurred, but prior calls failed to improve forward-year larval-detection prediction (Supplementary Methods S20). Neither result calibrates cohort abundance to calling or identifies natural predator effects.

### A specific prediction for field tests of vegetation benefit

The model predicts that vegetation benefit depends jointly on predator arrival and aquatic duration when shelter and feeding functions coexist. Testing this prediction requires actual odonate and caudate larval density and size, frog larval counts and stage transitions, submerged vegetation refuges, hydroperiod and constrained predation estimates. Subsequent breeding participation and repeated calls would connect these demographic responses to acoustic observations. A calibrated observation model and geographic holdouts would then test whether the mechanism predicts natural outcomes. The present study establishes the reversal within the stipulated food web; the field associations motivate this test without identifying its causal pathway.

## Conclusion

**Food webs that reproduce idealized frog–predator association signs need not benefit the frogs in those webs.** Across the full 27-cell hypothetical rate grid, the two vegetation functions increased sign compatibility on average while decreasing aquatic frog-cohort abundance; the positive nonadditive signature effect was restricted to particular rates. Developmental context changed the demographic outcome: early salamander arrival and prolonged aquatic exposure jointly reversed vegetation's effect in the independently controlled P1 experiment, and the original early/long context also showed a positive average across the broader rate grid. Pattern compatibility, population size and conditional long-term occupancy therefore represent distinct outcomes. The continental acoustic association motivates the model but does not identify a unique natural trophic mechanism or validate a quantitative link between tadpoles and recorded calls.
