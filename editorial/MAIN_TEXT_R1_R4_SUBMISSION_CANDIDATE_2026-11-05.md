# Third-guild context in frog calling and divergent outcomes of vegetation-mediated predator pathways

**Submission main-text candidate · 5 November 2026 · source-backed research draft · not submitted or coauthor-approved**

## Abstract

Pairwise species associations can vary with third guilds without identifying the underlying trophic processes. We combined 4,248 repeatedly surveyed North American frog-calling locations with stage-structured food-web simulations to distinguish geographical context, interaction-pattern generation and population consequences. The odonate-suitability–frog-calling association differed between locations of high and low pond-breeding caudate suitability (high-minus-low Spearman contrasts: −0.385 under uniform and −0.179 under target-group distribution models). Spatial and acoustic observation-model sensitivities did not establish a background-robust predictive contribution beyond shared environmental variation. Separately, 794 of 20,000 original structure–stage–rate simulations generated four idealized interaction directions after 80 years; compatibility was more frequent under synthetic conditions permitting larval persistence. In a new independently sampled set of 256 canonical food-web backgrounds, we held other mechanisms and rates fixed while crossing vegetation-mediated frog refuge from salamanders with vegetation-supported odonate feeding. The simultaneous four-sign compatibility rate increased from 3.52% with neither function to 14.06% with both, with a positive nonadditive contrast (+4.04 percentage points; source-bootstrap interval +0.91 to +7.29). Odonates' mean source-level share of the two modelled frog-predation hazards increased from 59.7% to 73.9%, but combined vegetation functions reduced mean frog abundance. In 24,576 additional finite-population 80-year model scenarios using the same 256 backgrounds, net patch-occupancy gains were generally weak and changed direction across developmental contexts. Thus the generation of ecological interaction signatures, relative predator hazards and prey population persistence are distinct model outcomes. The geographical acoustic associations do not establish their causal trophic explanation.

**Keywords:** amphibian calling; caudate guild; dragonfly larvae; conditional associations; ontogeny; vegetation refuge; stage-structured food webs; finite-population persistence

## Introduction

Ecological relationships are often summarized as associations between two guilds or taxa. A negative correlation between a potential predator and prey, for example, may suggest trophic suppression, but its magnitude and even direction can vary with other guilds, habitat structures, phenology and geographic context. That variation matters because the same statistical association can arise from several different processes: direct attack, apparent predation, release from an intermediate predator, common environmental filtering or the observation process itself. A major challenge is therefore to move from a geographical pattern to a set of discriminating biological hypotheses without treating the pattern as evidence that one mechanism has been established.

Wetlands make this challenge tangible. Frog larvae can experience attack by odonate and caudate larvae; those consumers may also interact with one another. Body size, first arrival and the duration of the aquatic developmental stage can alter encounter opportunities. Aquatic vegetation may shelter frogs from salamanders, but structural habitat could also facilitate odonate feeding. These effects need not point in the same direction, and they need not produce the same consequences for geographical association statistics, larval abundance and long-term occupancy. A vegetation mechanism that makes a particular interaction signature more likely might even lower the focal prey's numerical abundance.

Large-scale acoustic surveys offer repeated information on frog breeding-associated activity but are an incomplete window on such food webs. Adult calling strength is neither a census of tadpoles nor a direct measurement of recruitment or predation. Likewise, species-distribution-model (SDM) suitability for odonates or salamanders measures potential environmental conditions, not the number of predators occupying an observed pond. This creates two linked research questions: whether the geographical association between odonate suitability and frog calls changes across potential caudate habitat, and whether stage-structured predator pathways provide interpretable *possible generators* of the corresponding directional patterns. A third question concerns the population consequences of those pathways once developmental state, finite population size and movement are allowed to vary.

We addressed these questions in four evidence layers. **R1** describes the observed geographical association and challenges it with geographic, shared-environment and acoustic observation alternatives. **R2** screens an original catalogue of food-web mechanisms across uncertain synthetic demographic rates and then performs an independent within-model factorial manipulation. **R3** asks whether the same plant functions redistribute modelled predator-specific hazards and alter frog abundance. **R4** follows those exact same newly sampled food-web backgrounds into a stochastic 80-year population model. The central distinction is deliberate: an observed association, a model's ability to generate its idealized interaction directions, and that model's demographic outcome are not the same estimand.

## Materials and methods

### R1. Observed acoustic association and alternative explanations

We used original North American Amphibian Monitoring Program (NAAMP) site and survey data from **4,248 repeatedly surveyed listening locations**, drawn from **63,180 completed surveys** distributed across 432 route components. Frog acoustic response was the fraction of completed visits on which at least one frog species was recorded at the strong-calling threshold. We constructed potential guild environment predictors from 28 eligible SDM-modelled taxa: 15 frogs, seven caudates and six odonates. The main odonate predictor (**O**) was mean environmental suitability for the odonate guild; **S** represented mean suitability for pond-breeding caudates. SDMs were evaluated using uniform-background and target-group-background specifications on the same underlying monitoring locations; these are sensitivity choices rather than independent field replicates.

We estimated Spearman correlations between O and the strong-calling response across low, middle and high S-suitability tertiles, comparing the highest and lowest groups. Original 1,000-draw route-cluster bootstrap intervals accounted for route clustering. A landscape vegetation proxy (**V**) summarized flooded vegetation within 3 km of each acoustic monitoring site. This proxy is not a pond-level inventory of submerged stems or refuge. We retained whole-region and within-route comparisons, including interaction terms for O×S, O×V, S×V and O×S×V.

To examine whether an observed conditional association carries independent predictive information, we preserved five spatially disjoint folds based on **56 two-degree route-centroid blocks**. Predefined ridge-binomial acoustic regressions included survey, climate, water, land cover and geographic controls, with or without O×S. More flexible alternatives added 21 predeclared nonlinear combinations of six environmental variables. All model scores were evaluated on spatially held-out locations, with geographic block-bootstrap uncertainty; no held-out acoustic outcome was used to select predictor sets.

We further distinguished the *recording* of any frog call from the *strength* of a recorded call. The original visits included **47,912 any-call** and **36,554 strong-call** events, with 66 sites lacking any detected call. A two-part statistical observation model separately predicted P(any call) and P(strong call | any call); their product predicted the unconditional strong-call probability. We compared this prediction with a direct strong-call model on the same spatial heldouts and fixed predictor designs. This procedure changes the representation of adult acoustic observations only; it is neither a calibrated detection/occupancy model nor a larval-to-adult demographic link. Full response-definition and spatial sensitivity methods are retained in Supplementary Methods.

### R2. Source-defined trophic mechanisms and independent vegetation-function manipulation

The original mechanistic space comprised **3,600 canonical stage-structured food-web models** encoding direct and indirect trophic links, vegetation functions, developmental timing and feeding responses. A genuine seasonal predator–prey model was embedded in an **80-year delayed-recruitment model**. For each model/parameter proposal, we evaluated eight source conditions formed by two vegetation levels and low/high caudate and odonate supply. The response was the mean aquatic frog cohort over the final 20 years. The original model-native four-sign criterion required **OS−, OV−, SV+ and OSV+** on the log aquatic frog response, using the frozen \(10^{-6}\) sign threshold. These signatures are *idealized directions* and were not an absolute calibration to the magnitudes of the NAAMP acoustic associations.

We reaggregated all **20,000 originally simulated structure–development–rate proposals**, reporting proposal denominators and signs in the complete 3×3×3 grid of synthetic attack coefficient (0.5, 1.5, 3), local recruitment fraction (0.25, 0.50, 0.75) and maturation conversion (2, 4, 8). A minimum 5% frog-and-salamander source survival criterion served as an artificial viability sensitivity. The low-attack/high-recruitment regime was examined *after* these original source results; its selection is exploratory rather than a rate range identified from field predation measurements.

To test the structural hypothesis without comparing different models that contain different hidden features, we next **froze a new sampling seed before inspecting any new result** and sampled **256 distinct legal original-catalogue backgrounds** without replacement. These backgrounds were chosen to permit the two specific vegetation features to be toggled while leaving all other model flags intact. Original hypothetical rates were held constant within each quartet, with attack=0.5 and local recruitment fraction=0.75 retained from the historical exploratory regime. Two features were crossed: **frog refuge from salamander attack** (\`cover_SF\`) and **vegetation-supported odonate attack on frogs** (\`support_DF\`). The four legal original-catalogue states were 00 (neither), 10 (frog refuge only), 01 (odonate feeding support only) and 11 (both). All three original developmental contexts were evaluated, yielding **3,072 genuine 80-year model proposals** and 24,576 vegetation×caudate×odonate source responses.

The prespecified contrasts were both-versus-neither compatibility (\(p_{11}-p_{00}\)) and nonadditive factorial compatibility (\(p_{11}-p_{10}-p_{01}+p_{00}\)). We used 10,000 bootstrap draws at the **256-background** unit; developmental contexts and four intervention arms remained paired. The auxiliary 5% minimum-survival result was reported alongside, not used to select favorable source backgrounds.

### R3. Directly auditable model predator hazards and frog cohorts

For the same 256 backgrounds, we retained the original seasonal integrated per-frog hazards from salamander attack (**S→F**) and odonate attack (**D→F**) under vegetation-present/high-S/high-D source conditions. We calculated the model odonate share separately within each source as \(H_{DF}/(H_{SF}+H_{DF})\), conditional on the denominator being defined, then summarized source-level shares. Importantly, the **mean of source-specific hazard shares is not the ratio of the two separately averaged hazards**. These hazards measure modelled per-larva attack pressure, not numbers of real tadpoles eaten. All vegetation-function arms had to produce identical biological source outcomes when vegetation was absent, a preregistered numerical negative control.

We also compared the same matched model backgrounds for mean aquatic frog log cohorts and the conditional four-sign success indicator. The two outcomes use different scales: a higher compatibility rate need not correspond to a larger frog population.

### R4. Same-background stochastic long-term population consequences

We carried *all* 256 newly sampled food-web backgrounds—not only the four-sign successes—into the unchanged stochastic finite-population version of the original annual model. The design crossed three developmental contexts, four vegetation interventions, two hypothetical count-per-density scales (**q=3,12**), movement OFF/ON and two stochastic process seeds, producing **24,576 genuine 80-year model arms** on an eight-patch artificial ring. The model retained delayed recruitment and **external immigration ON**. Hence a local 0→positive patch transition cannot be attributed uniquely to movement. PR160's original population kernel and all biological rates were retained.

Primary responses were year-80 occupied frog patches (0–8) and the matched vegetation factorial on this endpoint, examined separately for q, movement and developmental context. Secondary outcomes were years61–80 mean patch occupancy, mean frog count, local extinctions and recolonizations, and the difference of movement effects between plant states. Original stochastic streams were keyed partly by plant-arm draw IDs; equal biological rates and process seed labels do not imply identical random-number trajectories. We averaged the two process seeds within each source/background and estimated conditional Monte Carlo intervals using 10,000 resamples of the 256 original backgrounds. These intervals characterize synthetic model-source variation, **not confidence intervals on wild trophic or demographic parameters**.

## Results

### R1. Third-guild context is observable but does not identify trophic control

The geographical odonate-suitability–frog strong-calling association was weak in aggregate but heterogeneous across pond-caudate-suitability environments. The high-minus-low S-suitability Spearman correlation contrast was **−0.385** (route-bootstrap interval −0.509 to −0.240) under uniform SDMs and **−0.179** (−0.328 to −0.029) under target-group SDMs. Within routes, the corresponding contrasts were **−0.122** (−0.199 to −0.034) and **−0.072** (−0.183 to +0.023). Thus the uniform-background within-route pattern remained negative, whereas the target-group within-route interval included zero.

The O×S predictor did not yield a consistent spatially held-out improvement once more flexible shared-environment terms were included. For the direct strong-calling response, the incremental negative-log-likelihood gain per completed visit was approximately **−0.00009** under each background, both with 56-block intervals spanning zero. The observed regional contrast therefore cannot be interpreted as independent evidence of realised odonate or caudate larval predation.

The acoustic definition also mattered. Under uniform SDMs, the within-route high-minus-low contrast for strong calling was more negative than the corresponding contrast for *any* recorded calling: **−0.122 versus −0.057**, with their paired difference **−0.064** (−0.116 to −0.012). This difference was not supported under target-group SDMs. A two-part any-call × strong-given-any observation model modestly improved spatial prediction over a single direct strong-call model under additive environmental predictors (negative-log-likelihood gain per visit **+0.000415** uniform and **+0.000860** target group; both block intervals positive). Yet adding O×S to the flexible conditional strong-given-any model did not improve prediction in either background, and worsened it under target-group SDMs. The two-part diagnostic identifies acoustic *response-definition sensitivity*, not a calibrated biological connection to tadpoles.

### R2. The generator depends on recruitment, predation and combinations of vegetation functions

Of the 20,000 original 80-year model proposals, **794 (3.97%)** generated all four idealized interaction directions. The compatibility fraction was **421/6,555 (6.42%)** at synthetic attack=0.5 but **146/6,696 (2.18%)** at attack=3; it was **424/6,689 (6.34%)** with local recruitment fraction 0.75 but **106/6,607 (1.60%)** with fraction 0.25. The conjunction of attack=0.5 and local recruitment=0.75 yielded **179/2,245 (7.97%)**, compared with **11/2,228 (0.49%)** for attack=3 and recruitment=0.25. Across the entire 27-cell rate grid, the highest single cell instead included *intermediate* attack=1.5 (78/710, 10.99%); the relationship was not uniformly monotonic. Requiring the original artificial 5% minimum frog/salamander survival reduced all-source compatibility from 794 to **426** proposals, with **none of the 146 four-sign successes** under attack=3 passing that additional survival criterion.

Within the historical low-attack/high-recruitment region, source models combining \`cover_SF\` and \`support_DF\` generated the four signs in **106/675 (15.70%)** proposals, compared with **12/424 (2.83%)** when both flags were absent. Because these were different canonical models, this comparison alone did not identify the effect of switching the plant functions.

The prospective 256-background same-model intervention resolved that ambiguity at the model level. Across 256 structures and three original developmental contexts, compatibility was **27/768 (3.52%)** with neither function, **44/768 (5.73%)** with only frog shelter, **60/768 (7.81%)** with only odonate feeding support, and **108/768 (14.06%)** with both. The matched both-minus-neither increase was **+10.55 percentage points** (source-background Monte Carlo interval +7.68 to +13.67), while the **nonadditive factorial contrast was +4.04 points** (+0.91 to +7.29). These contrasts also remained positive under the source's artificial 5% survival sensitivity. However, the nonadditive effect differed by developmental setting: it was +7.42 and +7.81 points in contexts 0 and 1, but **−3.13 points** in the early-salamander context 2, where the negative interval did not remain distinct from zero after adding the 5% survival gate. Vegetation-function synergy was therefore not universal across source life histories.

### R3. Predator-hazard redistribution does not guarantee greater frog abundance

The original model's seasonal hazards responded in opposite directions to the two vegetation manipulations. Under the vegetation-present/high-S/high-D source corner, mean S→F hazard declined from **0.624 to 0.376** when both functions were present, while mean D→F hazard rose from **0.488 to 0.945**. The *mean of separately calculated source-level* D→F hazard shares increased from **59.7% to 73.9%** (paired source-background difference +14.22 percentage points, interval +12.90 to +15.61). At vegetation=0, the four treatments gave identical original model states and hazards, satisfying the numerical negative control.

Despite generating the four-sign pattern more frequently, the combined plant functions **reduced mean log aquatic frog cohorts** relative to neither function by **−0.0841** (source-background interval −0.1128 to −0.0557). Frog refuge alone increased that mean, while odonate feeding support alone reduced it. The combined net effect was negative in developmental contexts 0 and 1 but positive in context 2. A vegetation function that improves the likelihood of an interaction *signature* thus need not benefit the focal frog cohort. The hazard-direction effect is partly built into the model's mechanism definitions; its joint consequences for interaction geometry and frog abundance are the tested simulation outcomes.

### R4. Long-term patch persistence is stage-dependent and can diverge from both signatures and abundance

The same new 256-model backgrounds produced **24,576 genuine stochastic 80-year finite-population model arms**. Yet the strong R2 improvement in sign generation did **not** consistently translate into a net increase in occupied frog patches. The pooled year-80 both-minus-neither contrast was **−0.022** (−0.101 to +0.056) occupied patches at q=3 with movement OFF, and **+0.029** (−0.037 to +0.096) with movement ON. Under q=12, effects were near zero because approximately **96–98%** of the original source arms occupied all eight artificial patches at year 80. The factorial interaction on year-80 occupancy was nevertheless **+0.100** (+0.018 to +0.180) patches for q=3 with movement ON, while the *net* both-minus-neither gain still included zero.

Developmental context changed the direction of the long-run outcome. At q=3 with movement ON, both plant functions versus neither altered year-80 frog patch occupancy by **−0.021** (−0.107 to +0.070), **−0.086** (−0.176 to +0.006) and **+0.195** (+0.037 to +0.354) patches across contexts 0, 1 and 2. These contexts jointly change salamander arrival and odonate aquatic duration, so this is not a controlled experiment on arrival order alone.

Meanwhile, the combined functions **reduced years61–80 modelled frog count**, averaged across contexts: by **−5.32 individuals** (−7.20 to −3.54) at q=3 with movement and **−23.65** (−30.81 to −16.92) at q=12 with movement, summed across the artificial ring. Local recolonizations and extinctions could increase together, rather than signalling greater persistence. Movement itself raised model occupancy at low count scale, but the *additional movement advantage* associated with both vegetation functions remained uncertain: q=3 **+0.051** (−0.042 to +0.147), q=12 **+0.014** (−0.001 to +0.029) patches. Because external immigration remained on, these findings cannot identify movement as the sole cause of recolonization.

## Discussion

### Third-guild context is an observation, not a predator census

The observed high-minus-low caudate-suitability contrasts show that a pooled odonate-suitability–frog-calling association masks geographical heterogeneity. But geography and shared environment remain serious alternative explanations. In particular, the conditional association was less consistent within routes and across SDM backgrounds; a flexible environmental acoustic model did not gain stable held-out predictive value from O×S. The G2 and G2B analyses showed that the definition of an adult calling response matters and that a two-part statistical response can improve acoustic prediction slightly. They did not supply the missing biological link from tadpole dynamics to mature calling males. Therefore neither the regional rank contrasts nor a fitted calling model select a particular wild predator pathway.

### Habitat functions can favor pattern generation while penalizing a focal population

The original food-web models provide a concrete explanation of why statistical success, predator-hazard redistribution and prey persistence must not be conflated. Under a synthetic demographic regime allowing recruitment to partly offset predation, paired vegetation functions made the four idealized interaction directions more likely. A new independent sample of canonical model backgrounds supported a positive interaction between frog protection from salamanders and vegetation-assisted odonate feeding in two developmental contexts, while the third context showed antagonistic nonadditivity. The opposing hazard changes are consistent with the source model's encoded functions; importantly, their combination need not increase frog abundance or long-term occupied patches. The identity of the protected trophic recipient, the alternative feeding pathway and developmental context together determine the numerical result.

These simulation findings are conditional in three ways. First, the attack and local recruitment region was selected after examining a larger synthetic model prior and **was never inferred as the true biological rate range** from the acoustic data. Second, a common sign pattern can be generated by distinct food-web structures, and successful models are not a field-conditioned ABC posterior. Third, the stochastic long-run ring experiment uses artificial patch geometry, limited count scales, an external immigrant source and model-defined developmental contexts. Positive numerical counterfactuals within this model family demonstrate **possible consequences of the stipulated mechanisms**, not their realized magnitudes in monitored ponds.

### A falsifiable ecological bridge remains to be built

The next decisive test is not simply to add more simulations or accept more model configurations. It requires *commensurate field measurements*: actual odonate and caudate larval density and size, frog larval counts and stage transitions, submerged vegetation refuges, hydroperiod, direct or experimentally constrained predation and subsequent breeding participation or calling. A biologically calibrated observation model would then link these states to repeated adult acoustic records while accounting for survey and detection processes. Predictions should be evaluated on geographic regions not used to choose the mechanisms or rates.

Until that test is possible, the strongest general result is the separation of three levels of ecological inference: **where an interaction-like pattern is observed, which trophic pathways can generate its idealized directions, and what those pathways imply for the persistence of the focal population**. Their alignment is a testable hypothesis, not a prerequisite that can be assumed.

## Conclusion

A third-guild-dependent geographical association in adult frog calling coexists with multiple possible trophic mechanisms. In independent, same-background food-web simulations, two vegetation functions changed the relative hazard of alternative frog predators and nonadditively enhanced an idealized interaction signature. Yet they did not consistently increase frog abundance or year-80 occupancy, and their net consequences changed with developmental context. Thus **generating a context-dependent ecological signature is not equivalent to improving the persistence of the focal species**. The natural predator interactions responsible for the observed acoustic patterns remain unresolved.
