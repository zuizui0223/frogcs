# Third-guild context in frog calling and divergent consequences of stage-structured refuge

**UPDATED SINGLE-PAPER MACROECOLOGY → BIOLOGICAL MECHANISM DRAFT — 1 November 2026. Unsubmitted, not coauthor-approved. All synthetic mechanism findings are model-native. Original 31 October draft and main results remain preserved.**

**Updated evidence chain:** observation in 4,248 actual monitoring locations → main's original 3,600 structures → original-source 20,000-draw/80-year model-native screen (PR #160) → exact-draw 96-source plant/stage intervention → q-dependent finite-N population outcomes → separate actual-site six-statistic adequacy check (PR #158). Stage contexts and annual immigration assumptions differ in PR #159, which is a complementary source-verified scope check, not a numerically pooled replication.

## Abstract

Geographical associations between species may depend on other guilds without revealing the trophic processes that generate them. Across 4,248 North American frog-calling locations, odonate suitability–frog calling correlations differed between high- and low-caudate-suitability regions (−0.385 under uniform and −0.179 under target-group distribution models), and, within routes under uniform backgrounds, the strong-calling contrast exceeded the any-calling contrast. A two-part calling model improved statistical prediction in some designs, but the guild interaction added no background-robust held-out gain beyond shared environmental controls. Separately, 794/20,000 original stage-structured food-web simulations reproduced four idealized interaction directions after 80 years, especially under moderate predation and sufficient local recruitment. In an independent sample of 256 canonical food-web backgrounds, toggling frog shelter from salamanders and vegetation-supported odonate feeding while holding other mechanisms and biological rates fixed increased four-sign compatibility from 3.52% to 14.06%, with a positive factorial interaction (+4.04 percentage points; source-bootstrap 95% interval +0.91 to +7.29). Odonates' share of modelled frog-predation hazard rose from 59.7% to 73.9%, yet the combined functions reduced mean frog cohorts overall. We extended these same 256 backgrounds into 24,576 finite-population 80-year simulations crossed by developmental context, count scale and movement. Combined vegetation functions did not consistently increase year-80 patch occupancy; with movement at low count scale, their factorial occupancy interaction was +0.100 artificial patches (+0.018 to +0.180). The combined functions increased occupancy in the early-salamander context but reduced modelled frog abundance on average. Thus interaction signatures, predator-specific hazards and demographic persistence are distinct, context-dependent model outcomes. None establishes realized wetland predation or a field-calibrated long-term forecast.

**Keywords:** macroecology; freshwater food web; frog calling; caudate guild; odonates; vegetation refuge; ontogeny; trophic interactions; model-native ABC; stage-structured demography

## Introduction

Interactions among species are often interpreted through pairwise associations. Yet a pairwise relationship can change across places containing different other species, habitats or life-history conditions. A negative association between a potential predator and prey may reflect direct predation, indirect release by a third guild, seasonal habitat overlap, shared environmental filtering or uneven observation. Distinguishing these possibilities matters if associations are to inform explanations of community organization rather than simply catalogue patterns of geographical co-occurrence.

Freshwater breeding habitats offer a useful system because several biological roles can intersect. Frog larvae can be consumed by aquatic salamanders and odonate nymphs; these predators may in turn interact with each other, depending on body size and developmental stage. Aquatic vegetation can modify direct access to prey, protect different guilds and alter density-dependent feeding responses. These mechanisms make a simple prediction tempting: the geographical association between frog breeding activity and odonates could differ in places environmentally suitable for caudates, and the role of vegetation might be contingent on which trophic pathway is operating. Yet the same pattern could emerge from shared climate, wetland type, geographic structure or differences in what is detected.

Frog acoustic surveys add a distinctive but indirect observational axis. Male calling is associated with breeding activity and can be measured repeatedly across large regions, but acoustic indices do not measure larval abundance or reproductive success. Earlier exploratory analysis of the North American Amphibian Monitoring Program (NAAMP) found rainfall-linked chorusing concentrated at recurrent physical sites; this motivated an examination of which community and landscape contexts characterize strongly calling locations. That previous result is not an independent validation of the present study, which draws on the same monitoring programme.

Our first question is **whether a third guild alters the geographical association between frog calling and potential odonate habitat suitability**. We ask whether the association differs along the potential distribution of pond-breeding caudates and varies with landscape vegetation, under multiple distribution-model backgrounds and spatial controls. Second, we ask **which stage-structured trophic pathways can generate corresponding conditional patterns and how robust those pathways are to biological-rate choices**. Third, we test **whether a model-based prediction about dispersal remains stable when recruitment and finite population size are changed**. The third question tests the implications and limits of model-native refuge and demographic predictions, not a claim that simulated larval predation caused observed adult calls.

The study brings geographical evidence and mechanistic constraints into one research paper while maintaining their different units of inference. Its possible general contribution is **a way to discriminate context-dependent ecological patterns from overly flexible trophic explanations**, rather than an assertion that environmental suitability establishes actual predation.

## Materials and methods

### Continental acoustic and guild-environment dataset

We used the source-verified NAAMP subset with **4,248 repeatedly surveyed acoustic listening locations** (at least six surveys across three years) and 432 route components in its original broad-scale analysis. The response was the proportion of surveys in which at least one focal frog taxon reached CallingIndex ≥2. This is a site-level frequency of strong recorded calling, not occupancy, density or egg-to-adult recruitment. Physical listening sites are not automatically independent ponds; connected route components were considered in resampling and fixed comparisons. In a separate source-frozen observation-outcome diagnostic, we rebuilt each site's strong-call and any-call counts from the same 63,180 completed visit records, exactly replaying the original 4,248-site R1 contrast and 1,000-route bootstrap intervals. We compared three acoustic responses: strong per completed visit (primary), any call per completed visit (the same 4,248 sites), and strong per any-call visit among the 4,182 sites with at least one recorded any-call event. Predictor-only caudate tertiles, both distribution-model backgrounds and the original route-bootstrap seed were held fixed. Conditioning on a detected any-call event changes the eligible population, and these ratios are not independently estimated detection probabilities or larval population states.

We additionally performed a **source-locked two-part adult acoustic spatial prediction** using the same original 4,248 NAAMP sites and 63,180 completed visits. Fixed PR145 penalized binomial models (ridge 10) were fit on the original five spatial training folds (56 whole 2-degree route-centroid blocks) for `any/all visits`, `strong/any visits` among any-positive training sites and `strong/all visits` directly. The held-out factored strong-call prediction multiplied the first two training-fitted probabilities, without using held-out responses. Under both original SDM backgrounds we tested the four frozen G1 explanatory designs: additive O/S/V, additive plus O×S, a nonlinear shared-environment baseline with 21 predeclared terms, and that baseline plus O×S. Conditional strong-call scores used the **47,912 recorded any-call visits** as trials; unconditional strong- and any-call scores used all **63,180 completed visits**. We retained **32 source model scores, 24 predeclared 56-block paired comparisons and 33,984 original held-out site×background×design predictions**, with 999 source-block bootstrap repetitions. All original G1 direct strong-call score values were replayed, and the held-out binomial losses were independently re-summed from saved source predictions. This two-part construction is a statistical model of **adult acoustic observation states**, not a measurement or biological calibration of larval predator abundance, maturation or survey detection probability.

Potential freshwater guilds were assembled from original species checklists, GBIF occurrence records and interaction evidence in GloBI. Of 30 interaction-supported candidates, **28 taxa** met the distribution-modelling criteria: **15 frogs, seven caudates and six odonates**. Occurrence-derived environmental suitability was projected to monitoring sites under both **uniform-background and target-group-background** model specifications. The pooled guild measures summarize relative niche suitability within the retained modelled species, not actual wetland predator numbers, the entire local species assemblage or observed species interactions at those survey locations.

### Third-guild conditioning and landscape vegetation

We quantified the regional association between strong-calling frequency and modelled odonate suitability and compared it across **low versus high pond-breeding-caudate suitability**. Differences in Spearman rank correlation were assessed by source-prescribed route-level resampling. We separately examined a rank-standardized model with odonate, caudate and landscape-vegetation main effects, all pairwise terms and the three-way term.

Vegetation was a source EarthEnv wetland-vegetation landscape indicator aggregated within **3 km** of monitoring locations from approximately **1-km** pixels, with 1-km-neighbourhood sensitivity. It is not a field measurement of submerged stems, protected microhabitat or emergence surface. Results from the full region were kept distinct from associations measured **within routes**, where shared geography and environmental suitability are partly constrained. Because both SDM backgrounds and several spatial resolutions reuse the same source occurrences, their agreement is a sensitivity result rather than independent external replication.

### Water history and prior use: supplementary observational context

Within source-matched strong-versus-silent site comparisons, we retained **4,040 pairs** for strictly prior strong-calling history and **3,907 pairs** with defined preceding-year satellite water-detection frequency. The latter is conditional on pixels where water had been detected and is separate from the fraction of nearby area ever detected as water; it is **not** a direct measurement of pond hydroperiod. These are secondary context measurements with different eligibility denominators. We do not use them to infer realized predator pathways or a demographic recruitment process.

### Competing direct and indirect trophic mechanisms

We compared mechanism families in the original F (frog larvae), S (salamander larvae) and D (odonate nymph) seasonal trophic kernel. Candidate pathways distinguish **S→F and D→F direct predation** from the **D→S→F indirect pathway** by which D can suppress an intermediate predator of F. S→D provides a contrasting directed interaction, and frog predation on emerging odonates is considered only in source developmental contexts where the stages overlap. We separately represented vegetation-mediated refuge for F or S, changes in odonate functional response, size-dependent risk and the original developmental timing/feeding combinations. Disabling an edge invalidates a plant-refuge role that acts only through that edge; nominally different but response-equivalent network variants were not treated as independent mechanisms.

The existing main screen comprised **3,600 unique trophic structures** followed by **20 candidate configurations ×24 shared sensitivity settings**, reporting both fulfilled and failed hazard and pairwise association criteria. The additional model-native ABC evaluated **12 structures ×256 hypothetical biological-rate vectors ×two reverse-predation flags**, and another vegetation/stage analysis consolidated **108 canonical response classes**, with explicit structural-prior dependence.

The stronger field-pattern assessment projected archived model-derived frog cohort surfaces onto the **same 4,248 observed NAAMP sites** using fixed rank mappings of actual Odonata and Caudata environmental suitability and vegetation. Its primary target comprised six **continuous** empirical statistics: O×S, O×V, S×V, O×S×V rank-regression coefficients and low- versus high-S subset O–frog-calling correlations computed using **global ranks**. Uncertainty and covariance were derived from source resampling across 56 geographical blocks with 999 draws. Six marginal descriptive 95% intervals are not a single calibrated joint test, and the source's globally ranked subset correlations differ from separate stratum-specific Spearman summaries. Model F larval cohort output was not calibrated to adult male strong calling or true pond-level predator density.

The process hypothesis families for prospective comparison are (i) a shared-environment/no-trophic-edge null, (ii) direct S→F and/or D→F predation, (iii) their addition to D→S→F and S→D links, (iv) edge-specific vegetation/refuge roles and (v) developmental timing and feeding effects. Proper source-matched comparisons require **exactly the same biological-rate parameters θ, plant settings and site observation mapping** before and after each edge deletion. The original fixed 132 site-mapped candidates and the larger 256-rate model-native library have not yet been connected into such a jointly field-adequate biological-rate posterior.

Earlier finite-N extensions compared movement, recruitment, edge deletions and the arbitrary count-per-density scales q=3,6,9,12 under related original 120-day seasonal and delayed 80-year cohort models. Their full exploratory grids remain Supplementary material; only the pre-linked, selected-source q3/q12 finite-population consequence is summarized in the main Results to test a different biological outcome, not to select the observed field trophic mechanism. With external immigration disabled, local recruitment depends on rho times conversion and does not identify those two parameters independently. No observed 80-year frog-larva occupancy or dispersal rate is available to treat the model endpoint as an independent field ABC target.

### Source-locked structure–rate comparison and matched demographic counterfactuals

The original main mechanism inventory contains 3,600 canonical models assembled from hypothesized direct and indirect trophic links, guild-specific refuge, feeding and emergence mechanisms. The original 20-finalist test assessed source-specific survival/hazard criteria and three rank interactions under 24 shared settings; it is distinct from the four-sign experiments below. The latter evaluated 20,000 seeded combinations sampled across the original 3,600-ID catalogue (3,582 distinct IDs actually drawn), three pre-specified developmental contexts and hypothetical attack, local recruitment, conversion, plant intensity and size-gating parameters. Each proposal ran the unmodified seasonal and lagged annual model for 80 years at eight original model-native V×S×D factorial corners. The last 20 years' mean aquatic frog density was evaluated using the log-scale OS−, OV−, SV+ and OSV+ sign criterion. Raw-scale and minimum-survival screens were sensitivity analyses. These categorical matches are prior-predictive generation events, not ecological posterior probabilities inferred from the NAAMP field data. Following the original full-source simulation, we independently regrouped all 20,000 proposals into the **27 complete combinations** of the three source attack coefficients (0.5, 1.5, 3), local recruitment fractions (0.25, 0.50, 0.75) and maturation conversion parameters (2, 4, 8), retaining denominators rather than counting only accepted proposals. Exploratory conditional regimes (attack 0.5 with recruitment fraction 0.75; attack 3 with fraction 0.25) were chosen after inspecting these priors and cannot be interpreted as confirmatory field-calibrated rate bounds. Within each rate regime, the original frequency of proposed structures supplies the denominator for model-native compatibility rates. A minimum 5% F-and-S survival threshold from the archived main ABC supplies an explicitly artificial viability sensitivity. These are evaluations of the same 20,000 simulations, not new biological simulations.

To test the resulting exploratory structural hypothesis independently at the model level, we froze a **new seed 20261123** before inspecting new simulation outcomes and sampled **256 distinct canonical backgrounds** uniformly without replacement among legal original-catalogue models with both targeted vegetation functions absent and without the behavior type incompatible with a shelter-OFF manipulation. For each background, we independently drew the remaining original hypothetical source rates (conversion 2/4/8, refuge strength 0.5/1, refuge order S/equal/D and binary original size gate), held `attack=0.5` and `rho=0.75` fixed as the post-exploration rate region, and ran the same 80-year seasonal and delayed-recruitment kernel under all three original developmental contexts. The two original intervention flags were separately crossed: `00` neither function, `10` frog protection from salamanders, `01` vegetation-supported odonate predation on frogs, and `11` both functions. Every counterfactual was verified to exist as a legal member of the original 3,600-model catalogue. The **3,072 model×context×intervention proposals produced 24,576 actual eight-corner source responses** and were compared within each original structure and rate vector, not pooled as independent ecological observations.

New-seed primary statistics were the model-native four-sign acceptance difference `11−00` and factorial interaction `11−10−01+00`, plus their independent minimum 5% F/S-survival criterion. To distinguish an interaction signature from a putative predator shift, we also read the original integrated per-frog `audit_H_SF` and `audit_H_DF` from the final 20 years at V=1, S_source=D_source=2, and quantified `H_DF/(H_SF+H_DF)` whenever defined. These are seasonal **per-larva hazards**, not actual wild predation deaths. All treatments at V=0 were required to produce identical original biological model responses as a negative control. We resampled the **256 independently drawn source backgrounds** 10,000 times with all three developmental contexts and all four treatment arms held paired. A separate independent source audit reconstructed all 24,576 original response vertices, canonical membership, hazards, acceptance and survival from the saved source files. This independent numerical replication does not independently sample wild ponds or verify that the historically selected rate values are ecologically realistic.

From the 794 source draws meeting the original 80-year four-sign criterion, 96 were selected with a frozen seed (32 in each original developmental context). Original-context sample weights reflected the 317/265/212 accepted-context frequencies. For each identical model ID and biological-rate vector, the effect of switching either vegetation-mediated frog shelter from salamanders (cover_SF) or salamander shelter from odonates (cover_DS) was evaluated ON versus OFF in all three contexts. Both change in mean log aquatic frog density and change in four-sign compatibility were retained, without selecting the better outcome after seeing the simulations.

We independently re-audited these 576 matched source contrasts by preserving the same source draw ID across all three counterfactual contexts and using 20,000 bootstrap resamples stratified across the 32 original selections per context. For a conditional exploratory link between R2 and the consequences in R3–R4, we additionally identified the **23 of the original 96 source draws** with attack=0.5 and recruitment fraction 0.75; **14** of these also had both vegetation-supported D→F predation and vegetation refuge from S→F. We preserved each exact source draw ID across the original paired refuge and stochastic 80-year result tables. Because the original 96 draws were sampled equally by their original context before the regime selection, this sensitivity reweighted the 23 source examples to the full 20,000-proposal R2 regime's original accepted-context counts (90, 65, 24), and the 14-model double-feature subset to 53, 42, 11. Source-level bootstrap confidence ranges were obtained from 12,000 stratified resamples. These small post-selection groups are exploratory model projections, not independent ecological replication. All estimates and intervals refer to **finite artificial source-sample Monte Carlo uncertainty**, not uncertainty about a real causal ecological effect. We additionally performed a post hoc survival-floor sensitivity retaining the 50 of 96 original selected source draws where both modelled frog and caudate survival exceeded 5% in the draw's original context (21, 24 and only five draws from original contexts 0, 1 and 2, respectively). The criterion does not ensure that each alternative counterfactual context is above the survival floor. A second sensitivity excluded 19 sources without an active D→S predation edge, in which S shelter from D is an exactly inactive intervention. These checks condition on model eligibility; they are not independent field replication. A complementary PR #159 analysis used the same class of seasonal trophic and finite-N models but only main's conditional candidates #1479/#1455, four hypothetical source rates and three *different* developmental source settings, with external immigration disabled. It was retained as a separate sensitivity study and not pooled numerically with this 96-draw experiment.

For a separate, prospective demographic consequence test following the new-seed 256-background plant factorial, we retained **all 256 originally drawn canonical backgrounds** without selecting those passing the four-sign test. The original PR160 integer finite-N stochastic kernel, eight-patch artificial ring movement (annual fraction 0.15), 80-year delayed recruitment and **external immigration ON** were unchanged. The identical 256 source structures and hypothetical nuisance parameters were crossed with three original developmental contexts, four plant interventions, q=3 or 12, movement OFF or ON and two new stochastic seed labels 311/313 (**24,576 genuine 80-year model arms**). Within-source population model stochastic streams remained independently keyed by original plant draw IDs: matching biological parameters and seed labels do not imply common-random-number trajectories. Results were grouped first by source ID and developmental context, with the two stochastic seed outcomes averaged per scenario, then uncertainty computed using **10,000 model-background cluster bootstrap resamples** of the 256 original structures; the repeated contexts, stochastic seed labels and eight patches were not counted as independent model backgrounds. Primary R4 outcomes were year-80 occupied frog patches and the 2×2 plant factorial, reported by q and movement; secondary outcomes were last-20-year mean frog counts, local 0-to-positive returns, local extinctions and movement effects. Source biodiversity and acoustic observations were not refitted.

For population consequences, the same 96 selected source IDs were carried into stochastic 80-year simulations with two count-per-density scales (q=3,12), S→F predation ON/OFF, movement ON/OFF and two process random seeds. Integer sampling, delayed recruitment and movement mass balance were checked within the model. The PR #160 finite-N version retained the original external immigration source, unlike PR #159's no-external-immigration comparison. Eight synthetic ring patches were model units, not observed wetlands or estimates of natural population density.

### Evidence and inference limits

The adult acoustic outcome, environmental suitability and landscape vegetation measured in the field are not larval density, actual predation and microhabitat refuge. Source signs, model abundance and simulated 80-year patch occupancy are different estimands; nor are the three developmental contexts identical between the original main screen and all later experimental extensions. A fixed site-to-model proxy map was separately tested with the six continuous observed-site statistics. Without an independently calibrated link between frog aquatic cohorts and adult calling, no directional or demographic success constitutes an empirically selected food-web mechanism. The source-rate Monte Carlo intervals and geographic route-bootstrap intervals quantify entirely different uncertainties.

## Results

### R1a. Observed third-guild suitability conditions the continental frog-calling association

Across 4,248 acoustic sites, regional Spearman association between odonate suitability and strong frog calling was **−0.095** under the uniform-background model and **−0.086** under the target-group background. The pooled coefficient alone is therefore small. However, conditional on pond-breeding-caudate suitability, the relationship varied substantially. In the uniform-background analysis, the odonate–calling correlation was **+0.101** in low-caudate-suitability locations and **−0.284** in high-caudate-suitability locations. The high-minus-low contrast was **−0.385** (95% route-bootstrap interval **−0.509 to −0.240**). Under target-group backgrounds the corresponding correlations were **−0.004** and **−0.183**, yielding a contrast **−0.179** (**−0.328 to −0.029**).

Thus the **contrast in association strength** persists across the two background specifications, although an apparent *change from positive to negative correlation* does not: the low-caudate group is approximately uncorrelated under the target-group model. Within-route and more tightly conditioned comparisons were less stable than the whole-region contrasts. In the original source-replayed sensitivity, the uniform-background within-route high-minus-low contrast was **−0.12185** [−0.19870,−0.03423] for strong calling, **−0.05749** [−0.13954,+0.02780] for any recorded calling, and **−0.13329** [−0.20729,−0.05491] for strong calling among recorded any-call visits (4,182 eligible sites). The paired difference between the first two source outcomes was **−0.06436** [−0.11593,−0.01165]. Under target-group backgrounds the within-route strong-minus-any difference was **+0.00341** [−0.05983,+0.06577], compatible with zero. Thus the geographic contrast is partly sensitive to *recorded adult calling strength*, but the difference is not background-robust and cannot establish breeding, predator control or a larval-to-adult demographic link. The geographic pattern is clear as a source-specific conditional association but remains compatible with spatially structured shared environments.

The new **two-part spatially held-out observation model** did not find background-robust incremental O×S prediction once nonlinear shared-environment terms were included. For **strong calling conditional on an any-call visit**, adding O×S changed out-of-block NLL per recorded any-call by **−0.000226** (56-block interval −0.000664 to +0.000180) under uniform backgrounds and **−0.000515** (−0.000946 to −0.000111) under target-group backgrounds; negative values indicate worse predictions. However, the factored `P(any) × P(strong|any)` model modestly improved upon direct strong-call prediction when both used the same original *additive* predictors: positive NLL gains per completed visit of **+0.000415** (+0.000105 to +0.000736) under uniform and **+0.000860** (+0.000307 to +0.001554) under target-group backgrounds. These small source-dependent improvements concern an **adult calling observation model**, not a true occupancy or larva-to-calling demographic process.

### R1b. Landscape vegetation and geography contribute context without demonstrating refuge use

The main 3-km landscape models gave odonate×caudate standardized rank interaction estimates **−0.166** (95% interval −0.229 to −0.108) for uniform background and **−0.086** (−0.151 to −0.023) for target-group background. Odonate×vegetation coefficients were **−0.087** (−0.124 to −0.048) and **−0.064** (−0.115 to −0.014). Caudate×vegetation point estimates were **+0.058** (+0.015 to +0.101) and **+0.046** (−0.022 to +0.109), respectively.

The three-way odonate×caudate×vegetation estimate was **+0.037** (+0.008 to +0.068) under uniform backgrounds but **+0.027** (−0.024 to +0.073) under target-group backgrounds, with spatial sensitivity. Accordingly, the positive three-way term **cannot be treated as a robustly established tri-trophic field interaction**. In separate fixed site comparisons, strong-calling locations showed **+27.68 percentage points** (95% interval +25.07 to +30.17) greater prior strong-calling history across 4,040 pairs, whereas preceding-year *conditional water-detection frequency* was **−1.481 percentage points** (−2.601 to −0.294) across 3,907 eligible pairs. Those estimates concern different observations and cannot be combined into a causal food-web coefficient.

### R2. Recruitment- and attack-conditioned food webs generate the interaction signature

In the original 3,600-structure screen, candidates #1479 and #1455 combined frog shelter from salamanders, odonate predation on salamanders and a vegetation-dependent odonate feeding response; the latter omitted S→D predation. The strict original rank/hazard criterion was jointly met in only one of 24 shared settings, and the original candidate's three-way rank interaction remained negative (−0.0482 or −0.0104), in contrast with the positive but background-sensitive uniform field point estimate (+0.037).

A new genuinely simulated 80-year structure–rate comparison sampled 20,000 proposals across the full main catalogue, rather than selecting only those 20 finalists. Exactly **794/20,000 (3.970%)** produced four idealized log-scale directional contrasts; 584/20,000 did so under the raw-scale alternative and 426/20,000 also met the original minimum-survival sensitivity criterion. At least 647 canonical model IDs generated a matching proposal. Notably, **272/6,608 proposals without S→D predation** generated the four signs, compared with 522/13,392 with that edge. Thus S→D predation was **not necessary in the sampled source model space**, although conditional acceptance fractions cannot be interpreted as a paired marginal effect of removing that edge.

The developmental context with the highest four-sign acceptance under the sampled nuisance rates was the original main context 0 (317/6,501 = 4.876%), followed by context 1 (265/6,827 = 3.882%) and context 2 (212/6,672 = 3.178%). This ordering differs from the source fixed-rate screen and illustrates structural and nuisance-rate dependence. Neither the four signs nor the size of the accepted model set demonstrates a causal explanation of the field survey.

The complete 27-cell rate reaggregation resolved a more specific conditional regime. Four-sign acceptance decreased across the three hypothetical attack coefficients, from **421/6,555 (6.42%)** at attack=0.5 to **227/6,749 (3.36%)** at 1.5 and **146/6,696 (2.18%)** at 3.0. It increased from **106/6,607 (1.60%)** at local recruitment fraction 0.25 to **424/6,689 (6.34%)** at 0.75; maturation conversion 2 and 8 generated **131/6,621 (1.98%)** and **383/6,672 (5.74%)**, respectively. The simultaneous **weak-attack/high-recruitment** regime (attack=0.5, fraction=0.75; all three conversions retained) yielded **179/2,245 (7.97%)** four-sign matches compared with **11/2,228 (0.49%)** under attack=3.0 and fraction=0.25. However, the highest of 27 individual rate cells occurred with *intermediate* attack=1.5, high recruitment=0.75 and conversion=8 (78/710, 10.99%); the extreme low-attack/high-recruitment/high-conversion cell was only 47/717 (6.56%). Neither a monotonic optimum nor a biological rate estimate is implied.

The original model-native **5% minimum survival** gate further distinguished sign generation from demographic viability: of 794 four-sign matching proposals, 426 remained. Under attack=0.5, 389 of 421 directional matches met the survival gate; at 1.5, 37 of 227 did, whereas under the strongest attack=3.0, **none of the 146 four-sign matches met the source-specific minimum survival condition**. This does not imply any species-level natural mortality threshold, but it makes the model's near-exclusion failure mode observable rather than merely conceptual.

Within the exploratory attack=0.5, fraction=0.75 regime, the presence of both **cover_SF** (vegetation protecting frogs from salamanders) and **support_DF** (vegetation facilitating D→F feeding) corresponded to **106/675 (15.70%)** four-sign acceptance, compared with **12/424 (2.83%)** when both structural roles were absent. Cases with only cover_SF and only support_DF passed in 32/693 (4.62%) and 29/453 (6.40%) respectively. Both-function source proposals remained strongly developmental-context dependent (53/221, 42/223, 11/231 for contexts 0–2). The candidate is therefore a **combination of opposing vegetation-mediated trophic pathways** in a survival-permitting model regime, not a universally required predation network. These rate-adjusted **between-model structural associations** do not isolate an independent causal effect of the support_DF flag and should not replace the matched source interventions reported in R3.

The independent new-seed matched test of this fixed-rate structural hypothesis was more discriminating. Across **256 independently drawn canonical source backgrounds**, all three developmental contexts and four vegetation-function arms, four-sign success was **27/768 (3.52%)** with both functions absent, **44/768 (5.73%)** with frog refuge only, **60/768 (7.81%)** with odonate feeding support only, and **108/768 (14.06%)** with both present. The matched both-versus-neither difference was **+10.55 percentage points** (95% source-background Monte Carlo range +7.68 to +13.67), while the **two-factor interaction** `11−10−01+00` was **+4.04 points** (+0.91 to +7.29). Imposing the original artificial 5% minimum F/S survival criterion retained a both-versus-neither effect of +10.29 points (+7.42 to +13.28) and factorial interaction +4.82 points (+1.82 to +8.07). The original 15.7% versus 2.8% observation was a **between-model** comparison with different structural priors; the new ratio of approximately four is a **same-background intervention contrast**, not a literal replicate of a structural-prior ratio.

The effect depended on developmental context. The both-versus-neither success increments were **+16.02, +13.28 and +2.34 percentage points** for original contexts 0, 1 and 2. Corresponding factorial interactions were **+7.42** (+1.56 to +13.67), **+7.81** (+3.13 to +12.89) and **−3.13** (−5.86 to −0.39) points. The early-salamander context therefore remained one in which both functions improved passage relative to neither, but their **nonadditive combination was antagonistic**, and that negative interaction was not robust to the auxiliary original F/S 5% survival criterion. These findings confirm within-model, rate-fixed context dependence, **not** a field-identified predator-switching mechanism.

### R3. Which trophic guild is sheltered determines the modelled demographic response

For 96 sources independently selected from accepted 80-year proposals and replayed under the same structure and rate parameters, sheltering frogs from salamanders increased mean log aquatic frog density in **96/96** source draws under *each* of the three counterfactual developmental contexts. Source-accepted-context-weighted mean ON−OFF changes were +0.0412, +0.0402 and +0.1071 (95% stratified source Monte Carlo intervals +0.0270 to +0.0581; +0.0254 to +0.0581; and +0.0773 to +0.1403). These are dimensionless log-density differences inside the model, not measured changes in frog counts.

Nevertheless, the model's four-sign gate did not simply track this demographic benefit. Its weighted ON−OFF success differences were +7.29, +8.12 and −3.76 percentage points, with 95% Monte Carlo intervals −4.37 to +18.76, −1.05 to +17.50 and −11.89 to +4.59 points. All three intervals include zero, and the paired contrast between the first and last developmental contexts also includes zero. Thus the sign changes are a **descriptive source-model pattern**, not a precisely identified general developmental sign reversal in this 96-source subset. Independently, a broader PR #146 within-model plant×developmental experiment found stronger stage-dependent changes in directional compatibility; its different developmental settings, prior and deterministic log-density estimand should not be pooled with this result.

In the same 96-source experiment, sheltering *salamanders* from odonates tended instead to reduce frog log-density, with weighted changes of −0.0064, −0.0031 and −0.0139 (Monte Carlo intervals −0.0109 to −0.0024, −0.0068 to +0.0001 and −0.0184 to −0.0099). The indirect effect is mechanistically compatible with protection of a predator that consumes frogs, although these source interventions did not independently estimate all components of predation mortality. This comparison gives habitat complexity a more informative role than a generic 'more vegetation protects frogs' interpretation: the **identity of the protected trophic recipient** matters.

A further source-frozen original-survival-floor analysis revealed an important selection dependency. Among the 50 original source draws with at least 5% modelled F and S survival, frog-from-salamander shelter still increased mean log frog density in all three contexts (+0.03483, +0.03477, +0.12156), while its four-sign passage differences were **positive in all three** (+22.18, +21.44, +7.91 percentage points; source-resampling 95% intervals +5.28 to +39.08, +7.90 to +35.72, and +1.13 to +15.06). Thus the earlier +,+,− passage point pattern in the full 96 sources **does not persist under the survival-floor sensitivity**, and an unconditional claim of a developmental sign reversal is unwarranted. This sensitivity filters each draw on its original developmental context, not all counterfactual contexts, and has only five accepted original-context-2 draws.

In contrast, **sheltering salamanders from odonates** among the 50 survivor-eligible sources in developmental context 1 simultaneously **reduced modelled frog log-density** (−0.00379, source Monte Carlo interval −0.00606 to −0.00170) and **increased four-sign passage** by +15.05 percentage points (+6.02 to +25.19). The corresponding context-2 density difference remained negative (−0.01266, −0.01763 to −0.00831) but its passage increase (+5.65 points, −1.13 to +13.18) was uncertain. Seventy-seven of the original 96 source structures had an active D→S link; the other 19 source structures yielded identically zero S-from-D shelter effects, a structural negative control. The divergence between demographic benefit and pattern compatibility is therefore supported for a particular conditional source set, without identifying the composition or causal explanation of actual wetlands.

Within the original 96-source experiment, **23 already-selected source IDs** fell in the exploratory R2 weak-attack/high-recruitment regime, and only **14** also had the joint cover_SF/support_DF vegetation structure. These IDs were preserved, not resampled from accepted outputs after seeing their long-run consequences. In the 23-source subgroup, frog refuge from salamanders increased mean log aquatic F cohorts by **+0.0577, +0.0618 and +0.1771** across the three counterfactual developmental contexts after weighting to the original 179 accepted R2 rate-regime sources. All 23 selected source draws had positive frog-cohort differences under all three contexts. However, the corresponding **changes in four-sign compatibility had source-bootstrap ranges crossing zero**. Thus within this narrower rate regime, demographic effects are easier to characterize than the directional signature of the full food-web response.

Crucially, the new independent 256-background experiment measured the original *predator-specific per-frog hazards* at a vegetation-positive and high-S/high-D source corner, rather than inferring a predator shift from four signs. Both vegetation functions together reduced average source integrated S→F hazard from **0.624 to 0.376** and increased D→F hazard from **0.488 to 0.945**; the D→F share of their sum rose from **59.69% to 73.91%** (paired +14.22 percentage points; source-background 95% Monte Carlo interval +12.90 to +15.61). The corresponding isolated source interventions gave ΔS→F hazard −0.2466 (−0.2816 to −0.2146) from frog refuge and ΔD→F hazard +0.4415 (+0.3943 to +0.4892) from odonate feeding support. At **V=0**, all other original F/S/D states, survival and predator hazard outputs were identical across the four vegetation manipulations, verifying that model-vegetation effects did not appear in vegetation-absent corners. The original model's source functions directly impose some hazard directionality; the substantive model finding is how their paired combination affects nonlinear pattern compatibility and life-history-dependent consequences.

The new independent matched experiment also **separated interaction-pattern success from population benefit**. Whereas the combined two functions increased four-sign acceptance, they reduced average source mean log aquatic frog cohorts from **0.444 to 0.360** (both-minus-neither **−0.0841**, source MC interval −0.1128 to −0.0557). Frog refuge alone increased mean log F cohorts to 0.563, whereas odonate feeding support alone reduced them to 0.251. The combined effect on frog abundance was not universally negative: both-minus-neither Δlog F was −0.1824 and −0.1864 in contexts 0/1 but **+0.1163** (+0.0780 to +0.1568) in context 2. In this latter context, odonates contributed ~31.5% of the two integrated frog-predation hazards with neither plant function but ~50.3% with both. This is a **modeled relative hazard redistribution**, not observed replacement of one predator by another in actual wetlands.

### R4. The same compatible source mechanisms imply conditional long-run persistence and movement

The same 96 selected model IDs and source rates were carried into 1,536 genuine 80-year finite-population counterfactual arms. With the synthetic count scale q=3, annual movement raised average year-80 frog occupied patches by +0.205 when S→F predation was absent and +0.196 when present. At q=12 the corresponding changes were −0.021 and −0.032 (out of eight artificial ring patches). The predation-specific moderation of movement was −0.009 (source Monte Carlo range −0.264 to +0.240) at q=3 and −0.012 (−0.069 to +0.041) at q=12. A robust generic S→F-specific dispersal rescue mechanism was therefore **not selected** by these counterfactuals; the near-complete q=12 occupancy ceiling also restricts interpretation.

In a separate four-rate, no-external-immigration study of the original main candidates (#1479/#1455), frog shelter from salamanders increased mean year-80 F patch occupancy in all three tested developmental source contexts, especially at q=12, whereas salamander shelter often reduced occupancy. Those settings were not identical to the three main source contexts used in the 96-draw experiment; this is *consistent-direction complementary evidence*, not direct numerical replication or a pooled effect estimate. Source-local reoccupancy events include delayed recruitment and within-ring movement; in PR #160 the external source is additionally active. They are not uniquely documented dispersal by real animals.

The source-ID linkage to the exploratory R2 rate regime also changed the interpretation of the movement experiment. In **23 selected weak-attack/high-local-recruitment sources**, the q=3 movement effect on F-occupied year-80 patches was **+0.665** with S→F predation present and **+0.135** when that path was removed. Their within-source difference, **+0.529** model patches (12,000 source-stratified Monte Carlo range **+0.154 to +0.898**), contrasts with the near-zero global 96-source mean. Under q=12, the corresponding moderation was only **+0.019** [0.000, +0.038]. In the subset also combining cover_SF and support_DF (14 sources), q=3 moderation was +0.558 [+0.038, +1.050], but only three source draws came from each of the first and last original developmental contexts. **These rate-conditioned model predictions are exploratory, selection-dependent and small-sample contrasts**, not an established general dispersal rescue effect or a real-wetland forecast. They show why the rate regime chosen in R2 can change the consequences read in R4.

**The prospective finite-N extension of the new-seed 256 canonical 2×2 experiment** independently connected R2–R3 plant manipulation to R4 on identical M×θ backgrounds, without selecting on source four-sign compatibility. All **24,576 true 80-year model arms** and 256 source IDs were recovered and independently audited against the original PR161 plant-intervention source. The source's final-year frog occupancy was bounded to eight synthetic patches; model **external immigration remained ON**, so zero-to-positive patch returns could be due to multiple sources.

Despite the **+10.55 percentage-point four-sign passage advantage** of both vegetation functions together in the identical new-seed model space, **their pooled effect on year-80 frog patch occupancy did not show a consistent increase**. Under q=3, both-functions minus neither was **−0.0221** [−0.1009,+0.0560] patches with movement OFF and **+0.0293** [−0.0371,+0.0964] with movement ON. The corresponding 2×2 factorial occupancy contrast was **+0.0176** [−0.0755,+0.1126] and, with movement ON, **+0.1003** [+0.0182,+0.1797] patches. Thus a positive source-conditional *nonadditive interaction* can coexist with a near-zero net population gain of the two functions together. Under q=12, occupancy was close to eight of eight patches, leaving tiny both-versus-neither changes: −0.0033 [−0.0163,+0.0098] with movement OFF and +0.0104 [+0.0007,+0.0215] with movement ON.

The source demographic effect reversed between developmental contexts. At q=3 with movement ON, both functions together changed year-80 occupancy by **−0.0215** [−0.1074,+0.0703] patches under context 0, **−0.0859** [−0.1758,+0.0059] under context 1, and **+0.1953** [+0.0371,+0.3535] under context 2 (early salamanders, with a concurrent change in odonate aquatic duration). Year61–80 mean patch occupancy was more clearly negative in the first two contexts and positive in the third (−0.0898, −0.0935 and +0.2549 patches, respectively, all three source Monte Carlo intervals excluding zero). Because these contexts jointly alter arrival and stage duration, these contrasts do not isolate the effect of arrival order alone.

In contrast to occupancy, the **last-20-year mean number of modelled frog individuals** decreased under the combined plant functions when averaged across contexts: by **−5.3247** [−7.2045,−3.5368] individuals at q=3 with movement and **−23.6539** [−30.8132,−16.9157] at q=12 with movement (outcomes are totals across eight synthetic ring patches, not actual censuses). This population-level reduction confirms that a model-native interaction signature can be easier to generate while focal abundance is lower, consistent in direction with the original deterministic R3 mean-log-cohort result. Under the early-salamander context, however, the combined functions increased mean frog individuals (+1.9074 [0.3624,3.4566] at q=3 with movement), again demonstrating life-history contingency.

In the new 256-source R4 experiment, **movement itself** increased q=3 year-80 patch occupancy under either plant state: +0.3359 [0.2754,0.3978] patches with both functions OFF and +0.3874 [0.3197,0.4564] with both ON. Yet the paired difference in movement benefit attributable to the plant combination was **+0.0514** [−0.0417,+0.1471], and at q=12 +0.0137 [−0.0013,+0.0286]; both ranges include zero. Therefore the new-seed 256 backgrounds **do not independently confirm a general vegetation-induced dispersal rescue**, despite the earlier exploratory 23-source contrast testing a different S→F-predation-specific moderation. The original population source includes external immigration. Reoccupation events and movement-associated reoccupation flags are not experimental field measures of actual frog dispersal. Conditional Monte Carlo intervals do not account for ecological prior uncertainty, model choice or multiplicity across the many population endpoints.

### Cross-level adequacy: directional source compatibility does not identify wild predation

A separate observational POM tested six actual-site statistics (O×S, O×V, S×V, O×S×V and low-/high-S global-rank odonate–calling correlations) using source block-resampling uncertainty. The earlier 132 fixed model surfaces produced **0 six-statistic matches under either SDM background**. A later same-biological-source 768-candidate finite-population proxy comparison found **0/768** covering all six descriptive marginal intervals under uniform backgrounds and **7/768** under target-group backgrounds; target-group matches were spread across models with and without individual direct predation links. No candidate satisfied both backgrounds, and the larva-to-adult calling map remained an unfitted proxy. In particular, the source directional acceptance subset of 794/20,000 is **not** a field-conditioned ABC posterior.

These results support a useful separation: (i) geographical third-guild-conditioned calling association is observed, (ii) varied food-web mechanisms can generate the simplified directional pattern, and (iii) a specific refuge may affect modelled frog abundance without reliably supplying the higher-order pattern or a universal long-run dispersal benefit. Direct evidence of larval predation at monitored wetlands is still needed to infer realized causal trophic mechanisms.

## Discussion

### Third-party context is a measurable feature of macroecological association

Our primary empirical result is not that odonates suppress frog breeding, but that a single pooled odonate–calling correlation conceals strong variation across the potential caudate guild. That conditional contrast is visible in two differently specified distribution models. A source-verified response-definition sensitivity found a stronger within-route contrast for strong than any recorded calling under uniform backgrounds, while the target-group background did not support the same difference. These are observed adult acoustic-event frequencies, not separately estimated presence, detection, fecundity or tadpole mortality. The additional independent G2B source-backed two-part acoustic spatial prediction found a small improvement when any-call occurrence and strong-given-any intensity were modelled separately in some fixed designs, but O×S offered no background-consistent incremental information beyond flexible shared environments. Thus neither G2 nor G2B supplies the missing biological F_B-to-adult-calling link. It supplies a rationale for incorporating third-guild context into broad-scale analyses of ecological relationships instead of treating every pairwise association as uniform across environments.

However, two properties limit its interpretation. First, the predictors are modelled **environmental suitability**, not co-observed larval predation or even pond-scale species abundance. Second, the relationship is strongest at the regional scale and becomes more uncertain within routes. A spatially varying shared habitat template remains a competing explanation, rather than a nuisance automatically excluded by an interaction coefficient. An upper-tier ecological claim would need to show which part of the contrast remains after explicit geographic and environmental decomposition.

### A protective intervention is not the same estimand as an interaction signature

The independent new-seed paired experiment strengthens this distinction: **the original plant-function combination generates idealized interaction directions synergistically in two developmental contexts, shifts relative predator-specific hazard, yet can reduce mean focal frog cohorts; an early-salamander context exhibits a negative factorial interaction on directional passage**. This is conditional model evidence, not biological confirmation that plants reassign natural predator dominance. The key ecological comparison distinguishes three questions that earlier mechanism screens sometimes collapsed: does vegetation alter a direct or indirect predation rate, does the altered food web generate the four directional statistical interactions, and does the simulated frog cohort persist in the long run? These are not interchangeable. In the matched 96-source analysis, frog refuge was consistently beneficial for modelled mean frog density, while its contribution to the four-sign selection gate varied and remained imprecise under source-draw resampling. Protecting salamanders produced an opposing density tendency, consistent with protection of a consumer of frog larvae. This identifies the **recipient of refuge and developmental opportunities for predation** as meaningful biological dimensions, not an empirically proven trophic mechanism at NAAMP monitoring stops.

The full 3,600-structure joint prior simulation provides constructive examples showing that S→D predation is not required for the simplified four-sign pattern. Its 3.97% acceptance rate quantifies a rare *model-native directional* outcome under the specified synthetic priors, not a probability that those ecological mechanisms are true. Original main's tightly conditional #1479/#1455 family, PR #146's broad plant-stage source comparisons, PR #159's four-rate no-immigration occupancy experiment and PR #160's 96-accepted-draw comparison therefore answer **different but complementary questions**. Comparisons of their numerical effect sizes require explicit alignment of developmental contexts, immigration process, source rate distributions and response scale. In particular, filtering the selected 96 mechanisms to the 50 original-context survival-eligible source draws changes the direction of some four-sign passage effects. This is not a contradiction of the previously successful numerical runs; it identifies viability and source selection as material conditions on claims of a stage-specific refuge effect. The most defensible mechanism insight is the non-equivalence of refuge-recipient effects on model frog abundance and on the four-sign pattern, not a universal sign reversal attributed to arrival order.

The source-prior result additionally suggests that interaction-pattern generation requires **a viable region of the model's attack and recruitment axes**, rather than simply an isolated required predator edge. However, this is not proof that weak trophic interactions stabilize the wild frog populations in the sense of McCann, Hastings and Huxel (1998), nor that actual tadpole losses are universally compensated by density dependence. Both field and experimental amphibian literature show species- and habitat-dependent density regulation (e.g. Loman 2004 versus Loman 2002). Our strongest ecological inference is therefore a conditional generator of patterns and their separate modelled consequences, not a measured predator regulation regime.

The strongest remaining obstacle is not insufficient simulation effort but **lack of a validated observation-process bridge** between field suitability/adult acoustics and model larval predator supply/frog cohort output. The six-statistic field fit failed to identify a stable unique cause. New results should not erase that mismatch, and source-draw bootstrap intervals are simulation Monte Carlo ranges rather than ecological confidence intervals.

### What could elevate a geographic pattern to a broad ecological principle?

The combined findings point to a testable programme rather than to a finished causal bridge. A discriminating field test would collect frog calling, larval frog and salamander sizes and counts, odonate nymph counts, aquatic vegetation and hydroperiod **in the same physical ponds and biologically appropriate seasons**, followed by subsequent recruitment. A formal observation model could then link process-model states to adult calling probability, survey effort and weather. In addition, the geographical caudate-conditioned odonate–calling contrast should be evaluated against predeclared shared climate, wetland and spatial autocorrelation controls in genuinely held-out regional units.

The general insight supported now is modest but useful: **ecological relationships inferred at one scale need not retain the same apparent strength or demographic meaning when third-guild context and life-history processes are considered**. The next evidentiary threshold is to distinguish third-guild-mediated interactions from coincident suitability and to test mechanistic predictions on commensurate data. Without that threshold, a broad conceptual mechanism should remain a falsifiable hypothesis rather than a demonstrated explanation.

## Conclusion

Across 4,248 repeatedly surveyed North American acoustic locations, the environmental association between odonate suitability and frog calling varies with potential caudate habitat. A broad original-kernel model comparison shows that diverse stage-structured food webs can produce simplified versions of this conditional pattern without any single required S→D trophic edge. Matched source interventions further separate **the recipient-specific demographic benefit of vegetation refuge** from **the model's ability to recreate a four-sign association pattern**. New-seed 256-background finite-population tests further showed that this same plant combination **does not consistently increase year-80 occupancy, reduces mean frog abundance overall, and changes sign across developmental contexts**. A positive low-q/movement-conditioned occupancy factorial does not imply an overall net population gain or a reproducible vegetation-dependent dispersal rescue. The geographical association is an observational result; the trophic explanation and long-run wild population consequence remain open. Our biological contribution is a **new-seed, same-structure demonstration that two vegetation-mediated trophic pathways can nonadditively change model-native interaction signatures while redistributing predator hazard and producing distinct focal-cohort outcomes**, within an explicitly uncalibrated attack/recruitment regime. This distinguishes *habitat protection, interaction signatures and population persistence*, rather than a claim that one predator pathway has already been identified.
