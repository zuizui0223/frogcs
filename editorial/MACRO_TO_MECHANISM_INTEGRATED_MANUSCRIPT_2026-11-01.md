# Third-guild context in frog calling and divergent consequences of stage-structured refuge

**UPDATED SINGLE-PAPER MACROECOLOGY → BIOLOGICAL MECHANISM DRAFT — 1 November 2026. Unsubmitted, not coauthor-approved. All synthetic mechanism findings are model-native. Original 31 October draft and main results remain preserved.**

**Updated evidence chain:** observation in 4,248 actual monitoring locations → main's original 3,600 structures → original-source 20,000-draw/80-year model-native screen (PR #160) → exact-draw 96-source plant/stage intervention → q-dependent finite-N population outcomes → separate actual-site six-statistic adequacy check (PR #158). Stage contexts and annual immigration assumptions differ in PR #159, which is a complementary source-verified scope check, not a numerically pooled replication.

## Abstract

Species associations are often interpreted in pairs even when their geographical patterns depend on other guilds and habitat conditions. We examined whether potential caudate habitat modifies the relationship between odonate environmental suitability and frog strong-calling activity, and whether corresponding patterns imply particular trophic or population consequences. Across 4,248 repeatedly surveyed North American acoustic locations and 28 distribution-modelled freshwater taxa, high-minus-low caudate-suitability contrasts in odonate–frog calling correlations were −0.385 (95% route-bootstrap interval −0.509 to −0.240) under uniform-background distribution models and −0.179 (−0.328 to −0.029) under target-group backgrounds. In a separate model-native experiment, we drew 20,000 structure–stage–rate proposals from an existing catalogue of 3,600 stage-structured food-web models. After 80 years, 794 proposals (3.97%) reproduced four idealized interaction directions, including 272 without salamander-to-odonate predation. For 96 accepted sources experimentally re-evaluated across three developmental contexts, sheltering frogs from salamanders increased modelled aquatic frog density in every matched source draw, although its contribution to directional pattern compatibility varied by context. Source-stratified resampling supported the density effect, while the changes in directional compatibility remained uncertain. Finite-population extensions yielded scale-dependent occupancy and no robust predation-specific effect on annual movement benefits. Separate site-mapped proxy comparisons identified no single trophic model adequately reproducing six observed associations across both distribution-model backgrounds. Thus third-guild-conditioned geographical associations coexist with distinct demographic and pattern-generating consequences of vegetation refuge, without identifying realized predator causation.

**Keywords:** macroecology; freshwater food web; frog calling; caudate guild; odonates; vegetation refuge; ontogeny; trophic interactions; model-native ABC; stage-structured demography

## Introduction

Interactions among species are often interpreted through pairwise associations. Yet a pairwise relationship can change across places containing different other species, habitats or life-history conditions. A negative association between a potential predator and prey may reflect direct predation, indirect release by a third guild, seasonal habitat overlap, shared environmental filtering or uneven observation. Distinguishing these possibilities matters if associations are to inform explanations of community organization rather than simply catalogue patterns of geographical co-occurrence.

Freshwater breeding habitats offer a useful system because several biological roles can intersect. Frog larvae can be consumed by aquatic salamanders and odonate nymphs; these predators may in turn interact with each other, depending on body size and developmental stage. Aquatic vegetation can modify direct access to prey, protect different guilds and alter density-dependent feeding responses. These mechanisms make a simple prediction tempting: the geographical association between frog breeding activity and odonates could differ in places environmentally suitable for caudates, and the role of vegetation might be contingent on which trophic pathway is operating. Yet the same pattern could emerge from shared climate, wetland type, geographic structure or differences in what is detected.

Frog acoustic surveys add a distinctive but indirect observational axis. Male calling is associated with breeding activity and can be measured repeatedly across large regions, but acoustic indices do not measure larval abundance or reproductive success. Earlier exploratory analysis of the North American Amphibian Monitoring Program (NAAMP) found rainfall-linked chorusing concentrated at recurrent physical sites; this motivated an examination of which community and landscape contexts characterize strongly calling locations. That previous result is not an independent validation of the present study, which draws on the same monitoring programme.

Our first question is **whether a third guild alters the geographical association between frog calling and potential odonate habitat suitability**. We ask whether the association differs along the potential distribution of pond-breeding caudates and varies with landscape vegetation, under multiple distribution-model backgrounds and spatial controls. Second, we ask **which stage-structured trophic pathways can generate corresponding conditional patterns and how robust those pathways are to biological-rate choices**. Third, we test **whether a model-based prediction about dispersal remains stable when recruitment and finite population size are changed**. The third question tests the implications and limits of model-native refuge and demographic predictions, not a claim that simulated larval predation caused observed adult calls.

The study brings geographical evidence and mechanistic constraints into one research paper while maintaining their different units of inference. Its possible general contribution is **a way to discriminate context-dependent ecological patterns from overly flexible trophic explanations**, rather than an assertion that environmental suitability establishes actual predation.

## Materials and methods

### Continental acoustic and guild-environment dataset

We used the source-verified NAAMP subset with **4,248 repeatedly surveyed acoustic listening locations** (at least six surveys across three years) and 432 route components in its original broad-scale analysis. The response was the proportion of surveys in which at least one focal frog taxon reached CallingIndex ≥2. This is a site-level frequency of strong recorded calling, not occupancy, density or egg-to-adult recruitment. Physical listening sites are not automatically independent ponds; connected route components were considered in resampling and fixed comparisons.

Potential freshwater guilds were assembled from original species checklists, GBIF occurrence records and interaction evidence in GloBI. Of 30 interaction-supported candidates, **28 taxa** met the distribution-modelling criteria: **15 frogs, seven caudates and six odonates**. Occurrence-derived environmental suitability was projected to monitoring sites under both **uniform-background and target-group-background** model specifications. The pooled guild measures summarize relative niche suitability within the retained modelled species, not actual wetland predator numbers, the entire local species assemblage or observed species interactions at those survey locations.

### Third-guild conditioning and landscape vegetation

We quantified the regional association between strong-calling frequency and modelled odonate suitability and compared it across **low versus high pond-breeding-caudate suitability**. Differences in Spearman rank correlation were assessed by source-prescribed route-level resampling. We separately examined a rank-standardized model with odonate, caudate and landscape-vegetation main effects, all pairwise terms and the three-way term.

Vegetation was a source EarthEnv wetland-vegetation landscape indicator aggregated within **3 km** of monitoring locations from approximately **1-km** pixels, with 1-km-neighbourhood sensitivity. It is not a field measurement of submerged stems, protected microhabitat or emergence surface. Results from the full region were kept distinct from associations measured **within routes**, where shared geography and environmental suitability are partly constrained. Because both SDM backgrounds and several spatial resolutions reuse the same source occurrences, their agreement is a sensitivity result rather than independent external replication.

### Water history and prior use: supplementary observational context

Within source-matched strong-versus-silent site comparisons, we retained **4,040 pairs** for strictly prior strong-calling history and **3,907 pairs** with defined preceding-year satellite water-detection frequency. The latter is conditional on pixels where water had been detected and is separate from the fraction of nearby area ever detected as water; it is **not** a direct measurement of pond hydroperiod. These are secondary context measurements with different eligibility denominators. We do not use them to infer realized predator pathways or a demographic recruitment process.

### Competing direct and indirect trophic mechanisms

We compared mechanism families in the original F (frog larvae), S (salamander larvae) and D (odonate nymph) seasonal trophic kernel. Candidate pathways distinguish **S→F and D→F direct predation** from the **D→S→F indirect pathway** by which D can suppress an intermediate predator of F. S→D provides a contrasting directed interaction, and frog predation on emerging odonates is considered only in source developmental contexts where the stages overlap. We separately represented vegetation-mediated refuge for F or S, changes in odonate functional response, size-dependent risk and the original developmental timing/feeding combinations. Disabling an edge invalidates a plant-refuge role that acts only through that edge; nominally different but response-equivalent network variants were not treated as independent mechanisms.

The existing main screen comprised **3,600 unique trophic structures** followed by **20 candidate configurations ×24 shared sensitivity settings**, reporting both fulfilled and failed hazard and pairwise association criteria. The additional model-native ABC evaluated **12 structures ×256 hypothetical biological-rate vectors ×two reverse-predation flags**, and another vegetation/stage analysis consolidated **108 canonical response classes**, with explicit structural-prior dependence.

The stronger field-pattern assessment projected archived model-derived frog cohort surfaces onto the **same 4,248 observed NAAMP sites** using fixed rank mappings of actual Odonata and Caudata environmental suitability and vegetation. Its primary target comprised six **continuous** empirical statistics: O×S, O×V, S×V, O×S×V rank-regression coefficients and low- versus high-S subset O–frog-calling correlations computed using **global ranks**. Uncertainty and covariance were derived from source resampling across 56 geographical blocks with 999 draws. Six marginal descriptive 95% intervals are not a single calibrated joint test, and the source's globally ranked subset correlations differ from separate stratum-specific Spearman summaries. Model F larval cohort output was not calibrated to adult male strong calling or true pond-level predator density.

The process hypothesis families for prospective comparison are (i) a shared-environment/no-trophic-edge null, (ii) direct S→F and/or D→F predation, (iii) their addition to D→S→F and S→D links, (iv) edge-specific vegetation/refuge roles and (v) developmental timing and feeding effects. Proper source-matched comparisons require **exactly the same biological-rate parameters θ, plant settings and site observation mapping** before and after each edge deletion. The original fixed 132 site-mapped candidates and the larger 256-rate model-native library have not yet been connected into such a jointly field-adequate biological-rate posterior.

Earlier finite-N extensions compared movement, recruitment, edge deletions and the arbitrary count-per-density scales q=3,6,9,12 under related original 120-day seasonal and delayed 80-year cohort models. Their full exploratory grids remain Supplementary material; only the pre-linked, selected-source q3/q12 finite-population consequence is summarized in the main Results to test a different biological outcome, not to select the observed field trophic mechanism. With external immigration disabled, local recruitment depends on rho times conversion and does not identify those two parameters independently. No observed 80-year frog-larva occupancy or dispersal rate is available to treat the model endpoint as an independent field ABC target.

### Source-locked structure–rate comparison and matched demographic counterfactuals

The original main mechanism inventory contains 3,600 canonical models assembled from hypothesized direct and indirect trophic links, guild-specific refuge, feeding and emergence mechanisms. The original 20-finalist test assessed source-specific survival/hazard criteria and three rank interactions under 24 shared settings; it is distinct from the four-sign experiments below. The latter evaluated 20,000 seeded combinations sampled across the original 3,600-ID catalogue (3,582 distinct IDs actually drawn), three pre-specified developmental contexts and hypothetical attack, local recruitment, conversion, plant intensity and size-gating parameters. Each proposal ran the unmodified seasonal and lagged annual model for 80 years at eight original model-native V×S×D factorial corners. The last 20 years' mean aquatic frog density was evaluated using the log-scale OS−, OV−, SV+ and OSV+ sign criterion. Raw-scale and minimum-survival screens were sensitivity analyses. These categorical matches are prior-predictive generation events, not ecological posterior probabilities inferred from the NAAMP field data.

From the 794 source draws meeting the original 80-year four-sign criterion, 96 were selected with a frozen seed (32 in each original developmental context). Original-context sample weights reflected the 317/265/212 accepted-context frequencies. For each identical model ID and biological-rate vector, the effect of switching either vegetation-mediated frog shelter from salamanders (cover_SF) or salamander shelter from odonates (cover_DS) was evaluated ON versus OFF in all three contexts. Both change in mean log aquatic frog density and change in four-sign compatibility were retained, without selecting the better outcome after seeing the simulations.

We independently re-audited these 576 matched source contrasts by preserving the same source draw ID across all three counterfactual contexts and using 20,000 bootstrap resamples stratified across the 32 original selections per context. All estimates and intervals refer to **finite artificial source-sample Monte Carlo uncertainty**, not uncertainty about a real causal ecological effect. We additionally performed a post hoc survival-floor sensitivity retaining the 50 of 96 original selected source draws where both modelled frog and caudate survival exceeded 5% in the draw's original context (21, 24 and only five draws from original contexts 0, 1 and 2, respectively). The criterion does not ensure that each alternative counterfactual context is above the survival floor. A second sensitivity excluded 19 sources without an active D→S predation edge, in which S shelter from D is an exactly inactive intervention. These checks condition on model eligibility; they are not independent field replication. A complementary PR #159 analysis used the same class of seasonal trophic and finite-N models but only main's conditional candidates #1479/#1455, four hypothetical source rates and three *different* developmental source settings, with external immigration disabled. It was retained as a separate sensitivity study and not pooled numerically with this 96-draw experiment.

For population consequences, the same 96 selected source IDs were carried into stochastic 80-year simulations with two count-per-density scales (q=3,12), S→F predation ON/OFF, movement ON/OFF and two process random seeds. Integer sampling, delayed recruitment and movement mass balance were checked within the model. The PR #160 finite-N version retained the original external immigration source, unlike PR #159's no-external-immigration comparison. Eight synthetic ring patches were model units, not observed wetlands or estimates of natural population density.

### Evidence and inference limits

The adult acoustic outcome, environmental suitability and landscape vegetation measured in the field are not larval density, actual predation and microhabitat refuge. Source signs, model abundance and simulated 80-year patch occupancy are different estimands; nor are the three developmental contexts identical between the original main screen and all later experimental extensions. A fixed site-to-model proxy map was separately tested with the six continuous observed-site statistics. Without an independently calibrated link between frog aquatic cohorts and adult calling, no directional or demographic success constitutes an empirically selected food-web mechanism. The source-rate Monte Carlo intervals and geographic route-bootstrap intervals quantify entirely different uncertainties.

## Results

### Third-guild suitability changes a weak regional two-guild association

Across 4,248 acoustic sites, regional Spearman association between odonate suitability and strong frog calling was **−0.095** under the uniform-background model and **−0.086** under the target-group background. The pooled coefficient alone is therefore small. However, conditional on pond-breeding-caudate suitability, the relationship varied substantially. In the uniform-background analysis, the odonate–calling correlation was **+0.101** in low-caudate-suitability locations and **−0.284** in high-caudate-suitability locations. The high-minus-low contrast was **−0.385** (95% route-bootstrap interval **−0.509 to −0.240**). Under target-group backgrounds the corresponding correlations were **−0.004** and **−0.183**, yielding a contrast **−0.179** (**−0.328 to −0.029**).

Thus the **contrast in association strength** persists across the two background specifications, although an apparent *change from positive to negative correlation* does not: the low-caudate group is approximately uncorrelated under the target-group model. Within-route and more tightly conditioned comparisons were less stable than the whole-region contrasts. The geographic pattern is clear as a source-specific conditional association but remains compatible with spatially structured shared environments.

### Landscape and water contexts add conditionality, not a confirmed field refuge mechanism

The main 3-km landscape models gave odonate×caudate standardized rank interaction estimates **−0.166** (95% interval −0.229 to −0.108) for uniform background and **−0.086** (−0.151 to −0.023) for target-group background. Odonate×vegetation coefficients were **−0.087** (−0.124 to −0.048) and **−0.064** (−0.115 to −0.014). Caudate×vegetation point estimates were **+0.058** (+0.015 to +0.101) and **+0.046** (−0.022 to +0.109), respectively.

The three-way odonate×caudate×vegetation estimate was **+0.037** (+0.008 to +0.068) under uniform backgrounds but **+0.027** (−0.024 to +0.073) under target-group backgrounds, with spatial sensitivity. Accordingly, the positive three-way term **cannot be treated as a robustly established tri-trophic field interaction**. In separate fixed site comparisons, strong-calling locations showed **+27.68 percentage points** (95% interval +25.07 to +30.17) greater prior strong-calling history across 4,040 pairs, whereas preceding-year *conditional water-detection frequency* was **−1.481 percentage points** (−2.601 to −0.294) across 3,907 eligible pairs. Those estimates concern different observations and cannot be combined into a causal food-web coefficient.

### The full catalogue permits four-sign patterns without S→D predation

In the original 3,600-structure screen, candidates #1479 and #1455 combined frog shelter from salamanders, odonate predation on salamanders and a vegetation-dependent odonate feeding response; the latter omitted S→D predation. The strict original rank/hazard criterion was jointly met in only one of 24 shared settings, and the original candidate's three-way rank interaction remained negative (−0.0482 or −0.0104), in contrast with the positive but background-sensitive uniform field point estimate (+0.037).

A new genuinely simulated 80-year structure–rate comparison sampled 20,000 proposals across the full main catalogue, rather than selecting only those 20 finalists. Exactly **794/20,000 (3.970%)** produced four idealized log-scale directional contrasts; 584/20,000 did so under the raw-scale alternative and 426/20,000 also met the original minimum-survival sensitivity criterion. At least 647 canonical model IDs generated a matching proposal. Notably, **272/6,608 proposals without S→D predation** generated the four signs, compared with 522/13,392 with that edge. Thus S→D predation was **not necessary in the sampled source model space**, although conditional acceptance fractions cannot be interpreted as a paired marginal effect of removing that edge.

The developmental context with the highest four-sign acceptance under the sampled nuisance rates was the original main context 0 (317/6,501 = 4.876%), followed by context 1 (265/6,827 = 3.882%) and context 2 (212/6,672 = 3.178%). This ordering differs from the source fixed-rate screen and illustrates structural and nuisance-rate dependence. Neither the four signs nor the size of the accepted model set demonstrates a causal explanation of the field survey.

### The recipient of vegetation refuge distinguishes pattern formation from frog abundance

For 96 sources independently selected from accepted 80-year proposals and replayed under the same structure and rate parameters, sheltering frogs from salamanders increased mean log aquatic frog density in **96/96** source draws under *each* of the three counterfactual developmental contexts. Source-accepted-context-weighted mean ON−OFF changes were +0.0412, +0.0402 and +0.1071 (95% stratified source Monte Carlo intervals +0.0270 to +0.0581; +0.0254 to +0.0581; and +0.0773 to +0.1403). These are dimensionless log-density differences inside the model, not measured changes in frog counts.

Nevertheless, the model's four-sign gate did not simply track this demographic benefit. Its weighted ON−OFF success differences were +7.29, +8.12 and −3.76 percentage points, with 95% Monte Carlo intervals −4.37 to +18.76, −1.05 to +17.50 and −11.89 to +4.59 points. All three intervals include zero, and the paired contrast between the first and last developmental contexts also includes zero. Thus the sign changes are a **descriptive source-model pattern**, not a precisely identified general developmental sign reversal in this 96-source subset. Independently, a broader PR #146 within-model plant×developmental experiment found stronger stage-dependent changes in directional compatibility; its different developmental settings, prior and deterministic log-density estimand should not be pooled with this result.

In the same 96-source experiment, sheltering *salamanders* from odonates tended instead to reduce frog log-density, with weighted changes of −0.0064, −0.0031 and −0.0139 (Monte Carlo intervals −0.0109 to −0.0024, −0.0068 to +0.0001 and −0.0184 to −0.0099). The indirect effect is mechanistically compatible with protection of a predator that consumes frogs, although these source interventions did not independently estimate all components of predation mortality. This comparison gives habitat complexity a more informative role than a generic 'more vegetation protects frogs' interpretation: the **identity of the protected trophic recipient** matters.

A further source-frozen original-survival-floor analysis revealed an important selection dependency. Among the 50 original source draws with at least 5% modelled F and S survival, frog-from-salamander shelter still increased mean log frog density in all three contexts (+0.03483, +0.03477, +0.12156), while its four-sign passage differences were **positive in all three** (+22.18, +21.44, +7.91 percentage points; source-resampling 95% intervals +5.28 to +39.08, +7.90 to +35.72, and +1.13 to +15.06). Thus the earlier +,+,− passage point pattern in the full 96 sources **does not persist under the survival-floor sensitivity**, and an unconditional claim of a developmental sign reversal is unwarranted. This sensitivity filters each draw on its original developmental context, not all counterfactual contexts, and has only five accepted original-context-2 draws.

In contrast, **sheltering salamanders from odonates** among the 50 survivor-eligible sources in developmental context 1 simultaneously **reduced modelled frog log-density** (−0.00379, source Monte Carlo interval −0.00606 to −0.00170) and **increased four-sign passage** by +15.05 percentage points (+6.02 to +25.19). The corresponding context-2 density difference remained negative (−0.01266, −0.01763 to −0.00831) but its passage increase (+5.65 points, −1.13 to +13.18) was uncertain. Seventy-seven of the original 96 source structures had an active D→S link; the other 19 source structures yielded identically zero S-from-D shelter effects, a structural negative control. The divergence between demographic benefit and pattern compatibility is therefore supported for a particular conditional source set, without identifying the composition or causal explanation of actual wetlands.

### Long-run population outcomes do not identify a dispersal mechanism

The same 96 selected model IDs and source rates were carried into 1,536 genuine 80-year finite-population counterfactual arms. With the synthetic count scale q=3, annual movement raised average year-80 frog occupied patches by +0.205 when S→F predation was absent and +0.196 when present. At q=12 the corresponding changes were −0.021 and −0.032 (out of eight artificial ring patches). The predation-specific moderation of movement was −0.009 (source Monte Carlo range −0.264 to +0.240) at q=3 and −0.012 (−0.069 to +0.041) at q=12. A robust generic S→F-specific dispersal rescue mechanism was therefore **not selected** by these counterfactuals; the near-complete q=12 occupancy ceiling also restricts interpretation.

In a separate four-rate, no-external-immigration study of the original main candidates (#1479/#1455), frog shelter from salamanders increased mean year-80 F patch occupancy in all three tested developmental source contexts, especially at q=12, whereas salamander shelter often reduced occupancy. Those settings were not identical to the three main source contexts used in the 96-draw experiment; this is *consistent-direction complementary evidence*, not direct numerical replication or a pooled effect estimate. Source-local reoccupancy events include delayed recruitment and within-ring movement; in PR #160 the external source is additionally active. They are not uniquely documented dispersal by real animals.

### Directional success does not establish quantitative adequacy at natural sites

A separate observational POM tested six actual-site statistics (O×S, O×V, S×V, O×S×V and low-/high-S global-rank odonate–calling correlations) using source block-resampling uncertainty. The earlier 132 fixed model surfaces produced **0 six-statistic matches under either SDM background**. A later same-biological-source 768-candidate finite-population proxy comparison found **0/768** covering all six descriptive marginal intervals under uniform backgrounds and **7/768** under target-group backgrounds; target-group matches were spread across models with and without individual direct predation links. No candidate satisfied both backgrounds, and the larva-to-adult calling map remained an unfitted proxy. In particular, the source directional acceptance subset of 794/20,000 is **not** a field-conditioned ABC posterior.

These results support a useful separation: (i) geographical third-guild-conditioned calling association is observed, (ii) varied food-web mechanisms can generate the simplified directional pattern, and (iii) a specific refuge may affect modelled frog abundance without reliably supplying the higher-order pattern or a universal long-run dispersal benefit. Direct evidence of larval predation at monitored wetlands is still needed to infer realized causal trophic mechanisms.

## Discussion

### Third-party context is a measurable feature of macroecological association

Our primary empirical result is not that odonates suppress frog breeding, but that a single pooled odonate–calling correlation conceals strong variation across the potential caudate guild. That conditional contrast is visible in two differently specified distribution models. It supplies a rationale for incorporating third-guild context into broad-scale analyses of ecological relationships instead of treating every pairwise association as uniform across environments.

However, two properties limit its interpretation. First, the predictors are modelled **environmental suitability**, not co-observed larval predation or even pond-scale species abundance. Second, the relationship is strongest at the regional scale and becomes more uncertain within routes. A spatially varying shared habitat template remains a competing explanation, rather than a nuisance automatically excluded by an interaction coefficient. An upper-tier ecological claim would need to show which part of the contrast remains after explicit geographic and environmental decomposition.

### A protective intervention is not the same estimand as an interaction signature

The key ecological comparison distinguishes three questions that earlier mechanism screens sometimes collapsed: does vegetation alter a direct or indirect predation rate, does the altered food web generate the four directional statistical interactions, and does the simulated frog cohort persist in the long run? These are not interchangeable. In the matched 96-source analysis, frog refuge was consistently beneficial for modelled mean frog density, while its contribution to the four-sign selection gate varied and remained imprecise under source-draw resampling. Protecting salamanders produced an opposing density tendency, consistent with protection of a consumer of frog larvae. This identifies the **recipient of refuge and developmental opportunities for predation** as meaningful biological dimensions, not an empirically proven trophic mechanism at NAAMP monitoring stops.

The full 3,600-structure joint prior simulation provides constructive examples showing that S→D predation is not required for the simplified four-sign pattern. Its 3.97% acceptance rate quantifies a rare *model-native directional* outcome under the specified synthetic priors, not a probability that those ecological mechanisms are true. Original main's tightly conditional #1479/#1455 family, PR #146's broad plant-stage source comparisons, PR #159's four-rate no-immigration occupancy experiment and PR #160's 96-accepted-draw comparison therefore answer **different but complementary questions**. Comparisons of their numerical effect sizes require explicit alignment of developmental contexts, immigration process, source rate distributions and response scale. In particular, filtering the selected 96 mechanisms to the 50 original-context survival-eligible source draws changes the direction of some four-sign passage effects. This is not a contradiction of the previously successful numerical runs; it identifies viability and source selection as material conditions on claims of a stage-specific refuge effect. The most defensible mechanism insight is the non-equivalence of refuge-recipient effects on model frog abundance and on the four-sign pattern, not a universal sign reversal attributed to arrival order.

The strongest remaining obstacle is not insufficient simulation effort but **lack of a validated observation-process bridge** between field suitability/adult acoustics and model larval predator supply/frog cohort output. The six-statistic field fit failed to identify a stable unique cause. New results should not erase that mismatch, and source-draw bootstrap intervals are simulation Monte Carlo ranges rather than ecological confidence intervals.

### What could elevate a geographic pattern to a broad ecological principle?

The combined findings point to a testable programme rather than to a finished causal bridge. A discriminating field test would collect frog calling, larval frog and salamander sizes and counts, odonate nymph counts, aquatic vegetation and hydroperiod **in the same physical ponds and biologically appropriate seasons**, followed by subsequent recruitment. A formal observation model could then link process-model states to adult calling probability, survey effort and weather. In addition, the geographical caudate-conditioned odonate–calling contrast should be evaluated against predeclared shared climate, wetland and spatial autocorrelation controls in genuinely held-out regional units.

The general insight supported now is modest but useful: **ecological relationships inferred at one scale need not retain the same apparent strength or demographic meaning when third-guild context and life-history processes are considered**. The next evidentiary threshold is to distinguish third-guild-mediated interactions from coincident suitability and to test mechanistic predictions on commensurate data. Without that threshold, a broad conceptual mechanism should remain a falsifiable hypothesis rather than a demonstrated explanation.

## Conclusion

Across 4,248 repeatedly surveyed North American acoustic locations, the environmental association between odonate suitability and frog calling varies with potential caudate habitat. A broad original-kernel model comparison shows that diverse stage-structured food webs can produce simplified versions of this conditional pattern without any single required S→D trophic edge. Matched source interventions further separate **the recipient-specific demographic benefit of vegetation refuge** from **the model's ability to recreate a four-sign association pattern**. Finite-population persistence is highly conditional on synthetic demographic scale and does not supply a robust predation-specific movement mechanism. The geographical association is an observational result; the trophic explanation and long-run wild population consequence remain open. Our biological contribution is a falsifiable distinction among *habitat protection, interaction signatures and population persistence*, rather than a claim that one predator pathway has already been identified.
