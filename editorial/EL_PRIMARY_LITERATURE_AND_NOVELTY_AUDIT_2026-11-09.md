# Ecology Letters first-shot — independent primary-literature and novelty audit

**Checked 2026-11-09; scope: the C (continental frog calling) → D (literature-grounded food-web counterfactuals) manuscript.** This is an editorial evidence audit; no biological fits, syntheses of effect sizes, new POM acceptance screens, or simulations were run. Literature outside the existing frogcs reference registry was searched by amphibian–odonate–caudate interactions, habitat complexity/refuges and predator perches, phenological overlap/priority, multiple-predator and trait-mediated indirect effects, pattern-oriented modelling/equifinality, population consequences, and recent competing studies. Entries were grounded in publisher abstracts, PubMed/NLM original abstracts or available article text; paywalled full texts were not assumed read. A searchable review of *selected relevant papers* is not a proof of worldwide absence of prior work.

## Executive editorial determination

**Supported synthesis, NOT a claim of first discovery:** Prior experiments directly establish frog tadpoles being prey to larval salamanders and dragonflies; plant/leaf-litter structure can shelter prey or facilitate predator attacks depending on spatial strategies; multiple predators can suppress one another or create indirect effects; seasonal arrival/size differences can reverse outcomes. Therefore there is **no novelty** in independently announcing any of those phenomena.

**Candidate paper-specific advance after that literature:** A single stage-structured synthetic food-web family tests **two non-equivalent responses to matched vegetation interventions**—the ability to generate four model-native analogues of contextual adult-call association signs, and modelled mean aquatic frog-cohort response—over 27 rate settings and two **disjoint** sets of 256 legal original structures. Pattern compatibility rises while mean model frog abundance falls (+2.705/+2.754 percentage points; −0.04030/−0.04125 log cohort). Within this family, predator arrival/duration P1 and an exploratory frog arrival calendar extension reveal that changing *relative* exposure times can reverse the **net demographic vegetation effect**. This particular conjunction is not reported in the comparison papers reviewed below. The lack of an observed wild model mechanism, field-calibrated stage response or across-guild causal test must remain explicit.

**Potentially strongest objection:** Wilbur & Fauth (1990), Wilbur (1997), Kishida et al. (2009), Davenport & Chalcraft (2013), Rasmussen et al. (2014), McCoy et al. (2012), and Baker et al. (2025) already show that food-web interactions, stage timing and prey survival can be context-dependent. Thus “multiple predators,” “indirect effects,” “predator timing matters,” “habitat complexity helps and hurts,” and “patterns are not causation” are **not novel takeaways**. The journal-facing claim must be narrower and genuinely tested: **matched habitat-pathway interventions decouple sign-compatible association-pattern generation from frog-cohort benefit, and changing predator–prey seasonal overlap can reverse which outcome improves.**

## Exact recommended first-shot paper language

**Novelty sentence (safe):**

> Experimental studies have established predator-specific shelter, habitat-supported foraging, and size-dependent priority effects. Here we test a different implication of combining those processes: whether vegetation pathways that increase the ability of stage-structured food webs to generate observed-like association signs also improve the focal frog cohort. Across independent model structures and synthetic rate regimes they need not; changing predator–prey developmental overlap can reverse the net vegetation effect.

**Essential limitations:**

- Adult calls are not documented tadpole births or recruitment. INBO/USGS B stage records have limited within-season recording association, failed future-year calibration; the 77.9-day same-site difference is **calling date**, not hatching.
- `cover_SF` and `support_DF` are imposed source interventions; hazard directions are partly definitional. The fraction 60%→74% does not demonstrate natural competitive release or trophic switching.
- The four source-native signs `OS−,OV−,SV+,OSV+` are analogous **directions**, not equal response coefficients for the continental adult acoustic data, not a field ABC posterior, nor an exact fit to adult-calling probability.
- Selection of the low-attack/high-recruitment historical source regime was exploratory; full 27-rate and disjoint 256 controls should anchor the main result.
- Conditional 40/78-day shifted tadpole stage simulations do not establish measured arrival differences in NAAMP, and new field voucher co-detections establish opportunities for encounter, not duration, attacks or vegetation benefit.
- Main G9 571/782 negative subsets are descriptive; rate/development matched G10 finds no clear **excess** loss in successful-sign models. Avoid “sign-generating food webs are intrinsically more harmful.”
- None of these literature findings can supply a validated **per-species** time-specific contact rate, attack coefficient, or natural pond parameter set for the original 80-year simulator.

## Editorial decisions following audit

1. Retain **C empirical context → D controlled process comparison** rather than requiring identical response variables.
2. Main: cite **Caldwell 1980, Wilbur & Fauth 1990, Klecka & Boukal 2014, Davenport 2013, Mocq 2021, Rasmussen 2014, McCoy 2012, Baker 2025** as direct contrasts; introduce recent Urushihara & Takatsu 2026 for size windows, if relevant.
3. Cover letter must compare to **actual closest prior papers**; do not exaggerate “first time.” Author/coauthor prior-publication disclosure is a separate unresolved editorial gate.
4. Preserve 27-rate G8/G11, stage P1 plus independent hypothetical frog-offset sensitivity as **models**, and observed species/calling stage/voucher findings in SI without pretending they calibrated same wetland-stage processes.
5. **No new field mechanisms or biological simulations** are necessary to execute this literature review. Unresolved before submission: all-author consent, project data/code archive DOI, third-party license permissions, reviewer access, figure proofs, source/quote QA.

## Search methods / source limitations

Targeted primary searches conducted 2026-11-09 across search engines and indexed PubMed, publisher platforms (*Oecologia*, *Ecology*, *Journal of Animal Ecology*, *Ecology Letters*, *American Naturalist*, *Hydrobiologia*, *Freshwater Biology*, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*), USGS article records and Dryad datasets, with query strings covering frog, salamander, dragonfly, multiple predators, vegetated wetlands, trophic cascades, intraguild predation, habitat complexity, priority/phenology, and co-occurrence-to-network inference. Twenty-eight-plus original studies and relevant reviews were screened with DOIs; no inaccessible full text was silently treated as reviewed. Systematic completeness, citation network exhaustive screening, all 2026 in-press literature, and equivalence of detailed model parameterization were **not** established. Follow-up should be expert-author checks against the key comparator papers, not ever-more post-hoc simulation.


## Ecology Letters editorial assessment, 11 November 2026

The submission asks whether habitat conditions associated with adult breeding activity also improve offspring abundance. The broad contribution is the separation of geographic pattern agreement from demographic benefit, followed by a controlled test of the conditions under which the benefit changes sign. The two next-season contrasts explain different outcomes in separate interventions. Their conjunction has not been demonstrated in a field cohort.

| Reviewer question | Closest evidence and implications for this Letter |
|---|---|
| Can an adult calling calendar inform larval exposure? | Repeated pond surveys found correlated temporal overlap among frog species pairs at the adult and tadpole stages. Predator-specific larval overlap remains unresolved at the NAAMP stops. Documented shared-site calling dates motivate calendar shifts but cannot calibrate them. [Carroll et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/oik.09773) |
| Does suitability measure a trophic link? | Associations can arise through joint habitat use. The retained four-climate-variable plus water SDMs omit flooded vegetation; that removes a reused predictor but leaves environmental covariance. Within-route support is clearest for odonate by caudate and odonate by vegetation. The 44 named species describe observation coverage. The seasonal trait comparison has 22 eligible species and shared-site surface spawning differences remain uncertain. [Blanchet et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/ele.13525) |
| Does vegetation have the modelled effects in ponds? | The leaf-litter refuge experiment involved adult newts. Structure can aid a perching predator feeding on invertebrates; odonate–tadpole experiments also show that refuges can lower risk. Three-kilometre cover cannot identify those pond-scale functions. The model isolates the net effect of two plausible functions under changing calendars. [Davenport & Chalcraft](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.691), [Klecka & Boukal](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00442-014-3007-6), [Hossie & Murray](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00442-010-1568-6) |
| Could competition or recruitment history give similar patterns? | Experimental caudate predation can change competition within the tadpole assemblage. Wood frog breeding abundance varied with juvenile recruitment across years. The manuscript's contribution here is the matched entry-density comparison conditional on 1,925 selected scenarios; entry-density equalization does not establish a corresponding lag in calling. [Morin 1983](https://doi.org/10.2307/1942491), [Berven 1990](https://doi.org/10.2307/1938295) |
| Is reversal itself new? | Frog and odonate role reversal has direct field evidence in Australia. Stage-structured role-reversal theory also predates this study. Fig. S12 instead follows a matched one-season switch through emergence, next-season larval density and per-tadpole hazard. Emergence delay under vegetation is assumed; its field magnitude and the effect on recruitment remain open. [Beranek et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecy.4108), [Li et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apm.2021.11.029) |
| How general is the model result? | Replication over two disjoint food-web sets and 27 rate combinations supports the distinction between reproducing signs and benefiting frogs. The developmental reversal uses a fixed rate setting, and the density and emergence analyses use different scenario sets. Their model frequencies are conditional on design and selection. They do not estimate how often these mechanisms operate in North America. |

The main editorial risk is a reader interpreting the model output as observed causation or as a prediction of the calling response. State the measured response and the simulated response separately. Keep the survey-scale, four-sign result and the sign change in vegetation benefit central; use the two seasonal experiments to explain demographic consequences. The manuscript is a defensible but ambitious Letter. Stronger discrimination among mechanisms would require repeated predator and frog stage observations, pond vegetation and recruitment at the same waters through successive seasons. The author guidelines do not make a new field experiment a submission prerequisite. The project archive DOI and authors' prior-work comparison are outstanding. [Official guidelines](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/page/journal/14610248/homepage/ForAuthors.html)
