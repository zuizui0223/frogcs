# Food webs that reproduce frog–predator association signs need not benefit frogs

## Abstract

What can the distribution of breeding choruses tell us about the value of ponds for frog offspring? Across 4,248 North American listening sites, the association between odonate habitat suitability and strong calling was more negative where pond-breeding caudate suitability was higher or the surrounding landscape contained flooded vegetation. In stage-structured food webs, we tested two possible functions of vegetation: sheltering tadpoles from salamander larvae and supporting odonate attacks on tadpoles. Across 27 rate combinations, enabling both functions increased the frequency of four associations resembling the survey pattern by 2.71 and 2.75 percentage points in two disjoint model sets, yet reduced average frog cohort abundance. Longer overlap with salamander larvae allowed shelter benefits to outweigh odonate feeding costs. For a delayed tadpole cohort, shifting salamanders with it yielded a vegetation benefit, whereas shifting odonates yielded a loss. Relative timing can thus reverse the demographic consequences of habitat-mediated predation.

**Keywords:** amphibian calling; caudate guild; dragonfly larvae; conditional associations; ontogeny; vegetation refuge; stage-structured food webs; finite-population persistence

## Introduction

Broad geographical associations can suggest biotic interactions, but shared environments and observation processes may generate the same patterns (Blanchet, Cazelles & Gravel, 2020). Pattern-oriented modelling uses observations to motivate and constrain mechanisms, not to prove that those mechanisms occur wherever the patterns are recorded (Grimm & Railsback, 2012). Classic temporary-pond experiments already show that dragonfly and newt predators affect tadpole abundance, survival and metamorphosis in different ways (Wilbur & Fauth, 1990; Wilbur, 1997). Our question is more specific: **does a vegetation-mediated food web that generates analogous association signs also benefit the focal frog cohort, and can relative developmental timing reverse the sign of that benefit?**

We use adult choruses to locate breeding activity and ask what those environments might mean for offspring. Independent surveys support an association between earlier calling and subsequent tadpole records (Supplementary Methods S20 and S22). Estimating recruitment requires observations later in development.

The biological ingredients are supported by **different experimental systems**, not measured as one connected mechanism in the continental surveys. Frog tadpoles have been experimentally consumed by both larval salamanders and dragonflies (Caldwell et al., 1980). Dragonfly cues can drive tadpoles beneath litter, reducing attacks by **adult newts** (Davenport & Chalcraft, 2013), while vegetation can also facilitate attacks by perching aquatic predators on **invertebrate prey** (Klecka & Boukal, 2014; Mocq et al., 2021). Leaf litter changes dragonfly–tadpole functional responses (Hossie & Murray, 2010), while structurally complex artificial macrophytes can also diminish multiple-predator capture success (Warfe & Barmuta, 2004). Relative arrival and body-size differences affect interactions in pond organisms (Alford & Wilbur, 1985; Rasmussen et al., 2014). We first describe continental acoustic associations, then independently manipulate the model's two opposing vegetation functions and predator developmental calendars. The resulting **pattern-versus-demography comparison** is a new test of combining established processes, not a claim that either plant function was calibrated to wild tadpole deaths.

## Materials and methods

### Continental acoustic associations and geographic controls

We used NAAMP observations from **4,248 repeated listening sites**, **63,180 completed visits in 2001–2015** and 432 routes (Foreman et al., 2017). Calls were recorded under 44 individual species names and nine species-complex labels (Supplementary Methods S2). The response was the fraction of completed visits with strong calling by at least one taxon (36,554 visits). We estimated odonate (**O**) and pond-breeding caudate (**S**) suitability using uniform-background SDMs fitted with four climate variables and open water. We excluded flooded vegetation from the SDMs so that the 3-km flooded-vegetation measure (**V**) entered the acoustic analysis separately. V was rank-standardized for geographic interactions (Fig. 1) and coded as absent or present in the original field–model comparisons (Tuanmu & Jetz, 2014).

We estimated O×S, O×V, S×V and O×S×V across all sites and within routes, resampling whole routes for uncertainty. Original six-predictor models, alternative backgrounds, species-removal and geographic holdout analyses are documented separately in Supplementary Methods S2–S3 and S16–S19. Without paired predator censuses across listening sites, we used the mean relative suitability of six odonate and five pond-breeding caudate species to compare habitat contexts. Geographic cross-validation tested discrimination of species occurrences from background locations (Supplementary Methods S2). Larval occupancy during the calling season remains uncalibrated.

### Stage-structured food webs and controlled vegetation functions

The original mechanistic space comprised **3,600 canonical stage-structured food-web models** encoding direct and indirect trophic links, vegetation functions, developmental timing and feeding responses. A genuine seasonal predator–prey model was embedded in an **80-year delayed-recruitment model**. For each model/parameter proposal, we evaluated eight source conditions formed by two vegetation levels and low/high caudate and odonate supply. The response was the mean aquatic frog cohort over the final 20 years. The original model-native four-sign criterion required **OS−, OV−, SV+ and OSV+** on the log aquatic frog response, using the frozen \(10^{-6}\) sign threshold. The rejection ABC-style screen targets idealized model signs, **not a field-conditioned posterior for NAAMP calls**.

We reaggregated all **20,000 originally simulated structure–development–rate proposals**, reporting proposal denominators and signs in the complete 3×3×3 grid of synthetic attack coefficient (0.5, 1.5, 3), local recruitment fraction (0.25, 0.50, 0.75) and maturation conversion (2, 4, 8). An artificial 5% survival floor was retained. G12 reweighted all proposals under nine illustrative synthetic rate priors and varied sign thresholds and response scale (Supplementary Methods S14). The historically selected low-attack/high-recruitment region is exploratory, not field-calibrated.

To test the structural hypothesis without comparing different models that contain different hidden features, we next **froze a new sampling seed before inspecting any new result** and sampled **256 distinct legal original-catalogue backgrounds** without replacement. These backgrounds were chosen to permit the two specific vegetation features to be toggled while leaving all other model flags intact. Original hypothetical rates were held constant within each quartet, with attack=0.5 and local recruitment fraction=0.75 retained from the historical exploratory regime. Two features were crossed: **frog refuge from salamander attack** (\`cover_SF\`) and **vegetation-supported odonate attack on frogs** (\`support_DF\`). The four legal original-catalogue states were 00 (neither), 10 (frog refuge only), 01 (odonate feeding support only) and 11 (both). All three original developmental contexts were evaluated, yielding **3,072 genuine 80-year model proposals** and 24,576 vegetation×caudate×odonate source responses.

The prespecified contrasts were both-versus-neither compatibility (\(p_{11}-p_{00}\)) and nonadditive factorial compatibility (\(p_{11}-p_{10}-p_{01}+p_{00}\)). We used 10,000 bootstrap draws at the **256-background** unit; developmental contexts and four intervention arms remained paired. The auxiliary 5% minimum-survival result was reported alongside, not used to select favorable source backgrounds.

### Rate-wide evaluation and independent structural replication

We extended the original paired vegetation-function factorial without retuning the biological kernel or selecting successful model structures. All 256 previously sampled legal backgrounds, three original developmental contexts and all four vegetation arms (00/10/01/11) were crossed with **27 fixed hypothetical rate cells**: attack coefficients 0.5/1.5/3, local recruitment fractions 0.25/0.50/0.75 and maturation conversion factors 2/4/8. This yielded **82,944 genuine 80-year model scenarios and 663,552 environmental vertices**. We compared paired four-sign acceptance, artificial 5%-survival compatibility and log frog cohorts across all sources. Ten thousand matched-background resamples yielded model-conditional 95% intervals. G8 and its disjoint G11 replication, both independently source-audited across 64 shards, are documented in Supplementary Methods S10 and S13.

### Model predator-specific hazards and frog cohorts

For the same 256 backgrounds, we retained the original seasonal integrated per-frog hazards from salamander attack (**S→F**) and odonate attack (**D→F**) under vegetation-present/high-S/high-D source conditions. We calculated the model odonate share separately within each source as \(H_{DF}/(H_{SF}+H_{DF})\), conditional on the denominator being defined, then summarized source-level shares. Importantly, the **mean of source-specific hazard shares is not the ratio of the two separately averaged hazards**. These hazards measure modelled per-larva attack pressure, not numbers of real tadpoles eaten. All vegetation-function arms had to produce identical biological source outcomes when vegetation was absent, a preregistered numerical negative control.

We also compared the same matched model backgrounds for mean aquatic frog log cohorts and the conditional four-sign success indicator. The two outcomes use different scales: a higher compatibility rate need not correspond to a larger frog population.

### Separating salamander arrival from aquatic-stage duration

In the original three-context experiment, the early-salamander setting altered both salamander arrival and its aquatic duration. We therefore froze a **new 2×2 developmental intervention** before calculating outcomes, retaining the same original 256 legal model backgrounds, attack and recruitment values, and all four vegetation treatments. Frog arrival (day 20), frog aquatic duration (45 days), odonate arrival (day 20) and odonate aquatic duration (60 days) were held constant. Salamander arrival was varied independently (early day 0 versus late day 40) of salamander aquatic duration (short 40 versus long 60 days). This generated **4,096 genuine 80-year model scenarios** and 32,768 original vegetation×salamander×odonate source responses. Late/short and early/long reproduced the prior original contexts 1 and 2 exactly across 2,048 source scenarios and 16,384 original eight-corner responses.

Within each background, we computed the both-functions-minus-neither difference in mean log aquatic frog abundance and four-sign compatibility under all four developmental settings. The pure arrival and pure duration moderators were averages across the opposite factor; their interaction was the usual difference-in-differences. All original 256 backgrounds remained in the analysis; **10,000 source-background bootstrap resamples** retained all timing and treatment comparisons paired. This separates two *model inputs*, not actual pond breeding chronology. A separate response-blind GBIF egg-stage eligibility audit did not meet the preregistered taxon-coverage threshold for a field breeding-lead analysis, which was therefore not performed.

After completing P1, we decomposed its net two-function effect into refuge-only, feeding-support-only and nonadditive components. All 256 backgrounds and four timings were retained, with shared source-bootstrap indices. This post-hoc explanation also counted background-level sign reversals (Supplementary Methods S8b).

Long-run stochastic R4 analyses and the R5 virtual-observer identifiability sensitivity remain entirely in Supplementary Methods S6 and S21, respectively; original observation diagnostics G1–G7 and G14–G17b are retained there without converting failure to positive field validation.

## Results

### Frog-calling associations vary with caudates and vegetation

Across 4,248 sites, all four interactions retained their original directions after flooded vegetation was excluded from the suitability models (Fig. 1). The odonate–calling association became more negative with increasing caudate suitability (**O×S = −0.171**) or vegetation (**O×V = −0.086**). Vegetation shifted the caudate–calling association upwards (**S×V = +0.062**) and attenuated the negative O×S interaction (**O×S×V = +0.042**). All four geographic 95% route-bootstrap intervals excluded zero; within routes, only O×S and O×V retained intervals wholly below zero. Both herbaceous and woody wetland cover showed negative odonate interactions in annual 30-m maps (Fig. S9). Across sites, 11 of 13 forest-associated frog species showed a stronger negative shift in the odonate–calling association with increasing woody than herbaceous wetland cover (Fig. S10). In the original six-predictor sensitivity analysis, the vegetation interaction remained negative after removing *Pseudacris crucifer*, the most frequently recorded species. The caudate interaction was negative for *P. crucifer* and positive for *Acris gryllus* in both whole-region analyses (Fig. S6), but the species contrast was uncertain at the 317 sites where both were recorded. Within shared site-years, *A. gryllus* called on average 78 days later than *P. crucifer* (Fig. S7).

In the original six-predictor analysis, incremental O×S terms did not improve geographically held-out strong-calling prediction over flexible shared-environment alternatives in either background. The subsequent G5/G7/G14/G15 model-shaped field predictors also failed their prespecified dual-background discrimination gates, and the G17 numerical replay failed. C therefore establishes a contextual **acoustic association that motivates** stage-structured models, not evidence that natural predator pathways were identified.

### Food-web structure and vegetation functions shape pattern generation

Of the 20,000 original 80-year model proposals, **794 (3.97%)** generated all four idealized interaction directions. Compatibility was higher at attack=0.5 (**421/6,555; 6.42%**) than attack=3 (**146/6,696; 2.18%**) and at recruitment=0.75 (**424/6,689; 6.34%**) than 0.25 (**106/6,607; 1.60%**). The weak-attack/high-recruitment region yielded **179/2,245 (7.97%)** versus **11/2,228 (0.49%)** for strong attack/low recruitment; the highest individual rate cell had *intermediate* attack (**78/710; 10.99%**). An artificial 5% survival condition reduced accepted proposals from 794 to **426**, with **none of 146** four-sign successes under attack=3 passing it. The 794 accepted proposals spanned **647 canonical IDs** (G13 rate/context-conditioned null: **712**, p=0.0002); G12 rate priors shifted acceptance to **1.09–6.85%**, while log-sign epsilon=0.01 retained **267**.

Within the historical low-attack/high-recruitment region, source models combining \`cover_SF\` and \`support_DF\` generated the four signs in **106/675 (15.70%)** proposals, compared with **12/424 (2.83%)** when both flags were absent. Across the full prior, G13 found **support_DF=1** enriched by **17.47 points** after rate/context adjustment (familywise p=0.0002); these cross-model associations still cannot identify a wild causal mechanism.

The prospective 256-background same-model intervention resolved that ambiguity at the model level. Across 256 structures and three original developmental contexts, compatibility was **27/768 (3.52%)** with neither function, **44/768 (5.73%)** with only frog shelter, **60/768 (7.81%)** with only odonate feeding support, and **108/768 (14.06%)** with both. The matched both-minus-neither increase was **+10.55 percentage points** (source-background Monte Carlo interval +7.68 to +13.67), while the **nonadditive factorial contrast was +4.04 points** (+0.91 to +7.29). These contrasts also remained positive under the source's artificial 5% survival sensitivity. However, the nonadditive effect differed by developmental setting: it was +7.42 and +7.81 points in contexts 0 and 1, but **−3.13 points** in the early-salamander context 2, where the negative interval did not remain distinct from zero after adding the 5% survival gate. Vegetation-function synergy was therefore not universal across source life histories.

### Vegetation redirects simulated predation hazards without guaranteeing frog benefits

The original model's seasonal hazards responded in opposite directions to the two vegetation manipulations. Under the vegetation-present/high-S/high-D source corner, mean S→F hazard declined from **0.624 to 0.376** when both functions were present, while mean D→F hazard rose from **0.488 to 0.945**. The *mean of separately calculated source-level* D→F hazard shares increased from **59.7% to 73.9%** (paired source-background difference +14.22 percentage points, interval +12.90 to +15.61). At vegetation=0, the four treatments gave identical original model states and hazards, satisfying the numerical negative control.

Despite generating the four-sign pattern more frequently, the combined plant functions **reduced mean log aquatic frog cohorts** relative to neither function by **−0.0841** (source-background interval −0.1128 to −0.0557). Frog refuge alone increased that mean, while odonate feeding support alone reduced it. The combined net effect was negative in developmental contexts 0 and 1 but positive in context 2. A vegetation function that improves the likelihood of an interaction *signature* thus need not benefit the focal frog cohort. The hazard-direction effect is partly built into the model's mechanism definitions; its joint consequences for interaction geometry and frog abundance are the tested simulation outcomes.

### Independent model sets reproduce pattern–population divergence

The complete 27-rate paired experiment increased four-sign compatibility from **2.252%** with both vegetation functions OFF to **4.958%** with both ON: a mean increase of **+2.705 percentage points** (source-bootstrap interval **+2.021 to +3.410**), equally weighted over all rates and three developmental contexts. The increase remained **+2.619 points** (+2.054 to +3.226) when compatibility also required the artificial 5% survival floor. Nevertheless, the same interventions **reduced mean log aquatic frog cohorts by −0.04030** (−0.04918 to −0.03109). The pooled factorial nonadditivity was **−0.709 points** (−1.461 to +0.010), showing that the positive interaction at the historically selected rate regime was not general across rates.

Across rates, compatibility improved in **15/27** cells, declined in seven and was unchanged in five; nine and three pointwise intervals were respectively positive and negative (unadjusted). All nine weak-attack cells improved (mean **+7.827 points**), versus +0.521 at attack 1.5 and −0.231 at attack 3. Mean frog-cohort effects were negative in **all 27 cells** when contexts were pooled.

The developmental context altered that conclusion for abundance. Pooling equally across all 27 rate cells, the original contexts 0 and 1 had mean log frog-cohort effects of **−0.07227** (−0.08327 to −0.06103) and **−0.07384** (−0.08525 to −0.06217); original context 2 instead had **+0.02521** (+0.01507 to +0.03543). Context 2 was positive in 20/27 cells; these original contexts jointly altered developmental features. P1 independently separated arrival and duration at its fixed rate (Supplementary Table S8; see also Figure S3 for the pathway decomposition).

G9 counted **571/782 (73.0%)** newly sign-generating cases with lower frog cohorts; G10 found no clear rate×context-adjusted excess loss (**−4.93 points [−11.28,+0.83]**; S11–S12). In G11, **256 previously unused legal structures**, crossed with the same 27 rates, reproduced both pre-fixed directions: compatibility **+2.754 points [2.083,3.434]**, mean log frog cohorts **−0.04125 [−0.05018,−0.03202]**. Among **835** new-sign cases, **612 (73.3%)** lost cohorts; developmental context2 again showed a positive mean abundance effect (S13).

### Salamander development reverses the demographic effect of vegetation

The controlled developmental factorial resolved the previously confounded early-salamander result. Relative to neither vegetation function, both functions changed mean log aquatic frog abundance by **−0.1864** (source-bootstrap 95% interval −0.2266 to −0.1464) under late arrival/short duration, **−0.1860** (−0.2271 to −0.1463) under late arrival/long duration, **+0.0021** (−0.0264 to +0.0317) under early arrival/short duration and **+0.1163** (+0.0787 to +0.1560) under early arrival/long duration. Thus earlier salamander arrival alone largely eliminated the negative vegetation effect, but **a reliably positive abundance effect required both early arrival and prolonged aquatic exposure** within this model. Across the two duration levels, early versus late arrival increased the vegetation effect on mean log F by **+0.2454** (+0.1967 to +0.2976). Longer versus shorter duration increased it by **+0.0574** (+0.0438 to +0.0714), with a positive arrival×duration interaction **+0.1138** (+0.0880 to +0.1409).

Yet vegetation's increase in four-sign compatibility was greatest when the effect on frog abundance was negative. Both-functions-minus-neither compatibility increments were **+13.28, +10.94, +9.38 and +2.34 percentage points** across late/short, late/long, early/short and early/long settings, respectively. Advancing arrival reduced this compatibility advantage by an average **6.25 points** (−10.74 to −1.95), and lengthening duration reduced it by **4.69 points** (−7.23 to −2.15). The model condition that most strongly benefited frog abundance therefore provided the **smallest incremental gain in producing the idealized signature**. This further separates compatibility from demographic benefit, while leaving actual natural breeding priority unverified.

The post-hoc decomposition attributed the late/short-to-early/long shift to increased refuge benefits (**+0.1719**, interval +0.1422 to +0.2042) and reduced feeding-support costs (**+0.1476**, +0.1157 to +0.1829), partly offset by nonadditivity (**−0.0167**, −0.0248 to −0.0093). Together these produced a **+0.3028** shift in the net vegetation effect. Of 256 backgrounds, **124 changed from negative to positive**, none changed in reverse, and 73 remained negative. Thus the mean reversal represents a broad, heterogeneous response within the sampled model family (Supplementary Methods S8b).

Motivated by the 78-day difference in recorded adult calling at shared sites, we varied tadpole arrival independently of the observed dates. With a hypothetical 78-day tadpole delay, shifting only the early, long-duration salamander cohort to overlap with tadpoles made the combined vegetation functions beneficial (+0.380 in mean log frog cohort abundance); shifting only the odonate cohort made them costly (−0.287; Fig. S8).

## Discussion

### From geographic variation in frog calling to a question about offspring

A pond where frogs often call strongly is a plausible place to look for offspring. Does it also offer those offspring favourable conditions? Across listening sites, strong calling declined more steeply with increasing odonate suitability where caudate suitability was high or flooded vegetation occurred nearby. Pond experiments show that the joint effect of two predators can differ from their separate effects (Wilbur & Fauth, 1990; Kishida et al., 2009). Following a pattern-oriented modelling approach (Grimm & Railsback, 2012), we asked what happened to tadpoles when vegetation altered their exposure to both predators in food webs that could generate analogous associations.

The woody–herbaceous contrast showed no clear gradient with species’ forest use within survey routes (Fig. S10). Regional differences in which frogs occur may therefore contribute to the geographic pattern. Our aquatic model addresses how shelter and predation affect tadpoles; it does not test habitat use after metamorphosis.

The two field indices serve different biological purposes. Frequent strong choruses identify breeding activity and provide an indirect indication of where tadpoles may subsequently be found. Belgian tree frog counts support that interpretation across ponds, although the association with metamorph counts remains uncertain (Supplementary Methods S20, S22a). Predator suitability ranks the surrounding habitat by its similarity to environments with recorded odonates or pond-breeding caudates. Local larval presence, density and seasonal overlap remain unknown. Regional climate and open water can still covary with flooded vegetation. In geographic holdouts using the original six-predictor SDMs, the model-derived pattern outperformed all 199 rate- and development-matched comparison patterns under the uniform background and none under the target-group background (Supplementary Methods S17). These observations motivate the controlled model comparison: holding a food web and its rates fixed, we can vary one predator’s timing or one vegetation function at a time, interventions the acoustic surveys cannot isolate.

### The same vegetation functions can favour the pattern and reduce frog abundance

In the model, vegetation sheltered tadpoles from salamanders and supported odonate feeding on tadpoles. These **encoded pathways were biologically motivated**, not field-calibrated: Caldwell et al. (1980) demonstrated the relevant tadpole–larval-predator contacts, Davenport & Chalcraft (2013) documented reduced **adult-newt** attacks after tadpoles hid in litter, and Klecka & Boukal (2014) and Mocq et al. (2021) documented vegetation-assisted foraging on **invertebrates** rather than frog larvae. Indeed, Hossie & Murray (2010) found refuge-induced changes that reduced aspects of dragonfly predation. There is therefore no universal empirical rule that vegetation promotes odonate attacks on tadpoles. At high simulated supply of both predators, enabling both functions shifted the *mean of source-specific* odonate shares of per-frog predation hazard from 60% to 74% at one fixed setting (Fig. 2). The model imposed pathway directions; its net demographic and pattern-generation responses were computed consequences.

Enabling both functions within otherwise matched food webs made all four association signs more frequent while reducing mean aquatic frog cohort abundance. This divergence recurred in a second, disjoint sample of 256 structures across the same 27 rate combinations (Fig. 3). The result compares two responses to the same intervention. It provides no evidence that generating the signs itself causes a cohort to decline. After accounting for rates and developmental context, newly compatible cases showed no clear excess loss (Supplementary Methods S11–S13), and the positive nonadditive compatibility effect at a selected rate did not generalize across all 27 settings. Multiple-predator nonadditivity is known to depend on functional responses and depletion (McCoy et al., 2012); it is not a novel universal synergy here. Several food webs can produce the same signs, and their demographic consequences depend on which predator accounts for the remaining risk to tadpoles.

### Salamander timing determines when shelter outweighs feeding support

Arrival-order effects are well established in frog competition and size-structured predation (Alford & Wilbur, 1985; Rasmussen et al., 2014), and more recent experimental work shows that salamander size diversity can reorganize several trophic pathways (Baker et al., 2025). Our distinct prediction concerns **the sign of the net vegetation effect**, not the discovery of priority effects themselves. At the rates used here, late salamander arrival with a short aquatic period increased four-sign compatibility by +13.28 percentage points but decreased mean log frog cohort abundance by −0.1864 with both vegetation functions enabled. Early arrival with a short duration brought abundance effects near zero (+0.0021); early arrival with a long aquatic duration made the net effect positive (+0.1163), while the compatibility gain was the smallest (+2.34 points) among the four settings (Fig. 3).

The timing of overlap helps explain this reversal. In another controlled aquatic system, predator effects persisted after tadpoles grew beyond the direct consumption window (Urushihara & Takatsu, 2026), reinforcing the importance of stage windows without calibrating them here. Salamanders arriving on day 0 and remaining aquatic for 60 days overlapped with tadpoles from day 20 to day 60, compared with day 20 to day 40 when their aquatic period lasted 40 days. In the post-hoc decomposition of the late/short to early/long shift, shelter contributed a larger benefit and feeding support imposed a smaller cost. Both changes helped reverse the mean effect, although individual food webs differed. Of the 256 food webs, 124 switched from a cohort loss to a gain and 73 still showed a loss. At shared listening sites, *A. gryllus* called about 78 days later than *P. crucifer* on average. Yet its tadpoles were collected with salamander larvae on three June pond-days in Florida (Supplementary Methods S2c–d). Even a late-calling species can therefore encounter the predator from which vegetation might offer shelter. In an exploratory model comparison, delaying a frog cohort together with salamanders gave vegetation a net benefit, whereas delaying the same cohort together with odonates gave it a net cost (Supplementary Methods S8c). The collections establish an opportunity for shelter. Measuring its value would require following tadpoles through the weeks when each predator is present.

## Conclusion

Across the listening network, vegetation and caudate habitat suitability marked where odonate suitability was most negatively associated with strong frog calling. In model food webs, vegetation made analogous associations more frequent even as mean tadpole cohort abundance fell. The balance reversed when vulnerable tadpoles overlapped longer with salamander larvae: shelter then outweighed the cost of vegetation supporting odonate attacks. The field prediction is that vegetation can have opposite effects on offspring in breeding ponds depending on which predator shares their larval season.
