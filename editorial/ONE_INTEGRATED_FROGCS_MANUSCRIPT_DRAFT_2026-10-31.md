# Spatially structured frog calling across rainfall pulses and freshwater community contexts: limits of stage-structured demographic explanations

**Single integrated research manuscript — working draft 2026-10-31. NOT submitted or approved by coauthors.**

**Author details:** To be supplied and approved. Do not copy the personal author metadata of the archived RC6 package without authorization.

## Abstract

Frog choruses vary sharply across nights and locations, but the spatial arrangement of reproductive acoustic activity, its association with other freshwater guilds, and the demographic processes capable of maintaining local populations are distinct ecological questions. We addressed these questions in a single evidence-hierarchical study combining repeated North American frog surveys, occurrence-derived environmental suitability and stage-structured population simulations. In 4,236 matched wetter–drier acoustic comparisons spanning 53 taxa, calling often shifted from silence directly to strong chorus. Among 2,916 comparisons with historical site information, within-taxon multi-site concentration was 1.650 versus 1.353 under a route-cross-fitted comparator accounting for species rainfall response, prior site propensity, dry-state persistence and expected activation (Monte Carlo P = 0.000999). Separately, across 4,248 repeatedly surveyed acoustic locations, the weak regional association between strong calling and modelled odonate suitability varied with the suitability of pond-breeding caudates; the high-minus-low caudate contrast was −0.385 (95% route-bootstrap interval −0.509 to −0.240) under one distribution-model background and −0.179 (−0.328 to −0.029) under another. Vegetation interactions and preceding satellite water detections supplied additional but less stable context. Stage-structured models generated some observed interaction directions only under restricted hypothetical settings and failed to reproduce the positive empirical three-way term. In separate 80-year finite-population extensions, a growth-dependent sign reversal in the effect of dispersal on frog patch occupancy required a salamander-larva-to-frog-larva predation pathway in a selected low-count model; that reversal progressively disappeared as the synthetic count scale increased. We therefore identify structured, historically selective acoustic activity and context-dependent geographical guild associations while delimiting, rather than asserting, their trophic-demographic explanation.

**Keywords:** acoustic monitoring; anurans; community context; environmental pulse; habitat history; odonates; caudates; stage-structured predation; dispersal; stochastic demography

## 1. Introduction

Calling is a conspicuous component of anuran reproductive behaviour, yet a calling index at a survey stop is neither abundance nor reproductive success. Rainfall can raise calling activity substantially within a night. At the same time, some sites repeatedly support stronger chorus activity and different taxa respond across different parts of the same landscape. The amount of calling and the spatial configuration in which it appears are therefore potentially different aspects of reproductive-site use. The ecological challenge is to characterize this configuration without confusing behavioural detection, local demographic persistence and inferred environmental suitability.

Two questions can be evaluated directly using standardized repeated acoustic surveys. First, after controlling for species-specific rainfall responses and previous use of physical sites, does the realised response occur independently across sites or show additional within-taxon concentration across multiple locations? Second, are places with frequent strong calling associated with the modelled potential distributions of other freshwater guilds, and does that geographical association change with wetland vegetation and hydrological context? These two questions use related NAAMP monitoring material at **different units and denominators**. They are not independent replication studies.

Three aquatic groups provide a natural framework for the second question. Larval frogs may be prey for both odonate larvae and aquatic caudates; odonates and caudates may also affect each other depending on size and developmental stage. Vegetation could modify refuge use and capture rates without simply increasing or decreasing any predator density. Such interactions are well-motivated possibilities in freshwater food webs, but an occurrence-based suitability surface for odonates cannot be equated with nymph density at an acoustically monitored stop. Likewise, landscape vegetation is not a direct measurement of protective cover in a pond. Geographic association alone does not identify a trophic process.

A model can help determine what mechanistic explanations remain viable under explicit assumptions. But the appropriate test is more demanding than finding one parameter combination reproducing a few signs: explanations must be examined for their dependence on developmental timing, demographic recruitment, movement and finite population size, and should be reported alongside their failures. A model of larval survival and occupancy cannot explain adult acoustic behaviour unless an observation and maturation bridge is estimated or otherwise validated.

We therefore combine **three linked but non-equivalent evidence layers within one manuscript**: (i) rainfall-related chorus state changes and multi-site spatial allocation; (ii) continental-scale geographical associations among acoustic site use, guild environmental suitability, vegetation, water detection and prior site use; and (iii) process-model constraints on proposed trophic, developmental and demographic accounts. Our overall question is not whether a particular predator caused the observed calling pattern, but **which spatial patterns are observed, which ecological contexts accompany them, and which classes of demographic explanation withstand explicit counterfactual tests**. This distinction allows a single integrated ecological study without mistaking model feasibility for field mechanism identification.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Evidence layers and observational units

Both acoustic analyses used records from the North American Amphibian Monitoring Program (NAAMP), but had separate inclusion criteria, time scales and outcomes. The rainfall analysis compared matched route-runs and their species × listening-stop matrices; the macroecological analysis summarized repeatedly surveyed physical listening locations. Their sample sizes, uncertainties and effect estimates must remain separate. Neither provides direct records of larvae, true local colonization, parentage or egg-to-adult survival.

We treat source data, analysis specifications and model runs as separate evidence. The original JAE-oriented RC6 acoustic analysis and its submission-quality checks are preserved in the repository; its results are considered here as the first empirical analysis of the **same research programme**, not as a previously published independent experiment. The macroecological results are from the main research source and the numerical process results are from distinct synthetic model variants with explicitly stated control settings. All new editorial integration is secondary to those original validated results.

### 2.2. Rainfall-linked acoustic transitions and within-taxon spatial concentration

In the 2001–2015 unified-protocol period, NAAMP routes contained ten sequential wetland-associated listening stops with ordinal CallingIndex 0–3. A recorded CI2 denotes overlapping but individually distinguishable calls; CI3 denotes a full chorus in which individuals are not distinguishable. Multi-stop calling refers to expression across spatially separated stations surveyed sequentially on the same night, **not simultaneous calling by known individual frogs**.

The source RC6 analysis retained 7,848 eligible route-runs and constructed 4,236 wetter–drier comparisons across 53 taxa, 585 routes and 21 U.S. states. Source inclusion, matching and weather quality control are detailed in the preserved RC6 manuscript and supporting information. Changes in route richness, calling state and the number of sites involved per taxon were summarized without interpreting a CI0→positive event as true colonization.

The focal source endpoint used 2,916 matched comparisons with strictly earlier physical-site history. It tested whether acoustically recruited taxa were concentrated at multiple sites more strongly than a comparator allowing species-specific rainfall sensitivity, strictly prior taxon × physical-site calling propensity, dry-state persistence and total expected wet-state activation. Responses were route-cross-fitted to avoid directly training and assessing the same source-route signal. Simulated null distributions and a held-out rainfall × historical-site diagnostic constrained the interpretation. Analyses of prior strong-chorus site targeting specified where the deep spatial response was expressed.

### 2.3. Guild suitability and landscape context of recurrent acoustic activity

The separate macroecological study included 4,248 acoustic survey locations with at least six visits over at least three years, distributed across 432 route components in its particular geographical matching framework. The site outcome was the proportion of eligible surveys with CallingIndex ≥2 for at least one focal frog taxon. It is a **site-level strong-calling frequency**, not a species abundance estimate.

GBIF occurrence records and GloBI evidence were used to identify potential freshwater guild candidates; 28 taxa met the minimum distribution modelling requirement (15 Anura, seven Caudata and six Odonata). Their environmental suitability was modelled under uniform and target-group background designs, and aggregate Odonata and pond-breeding Caudata suitability was associated with strong-calling frequency. These two model backgrounds are sensitivity specifications of the **same occurrence sources**, not independent ecological replications. Source suitability was not converted into field larval predator counts. Geographical high-versus-low pond-breeding-caudate suitability strata were compared with route-component-level resampling, and region-wide relationships were distinguished from within-route patterns.

EarthEnv landscape wetland vegetation was summarized within 3 km of survey sites, with 1 km scale checks. Regression models distinguished guild × vegetation pairwise terms from a three-way interaction. Underlying raster vegetation indicates landscape cover, not measured stems, underwater refuges or plant-specific predator avoidance. Preceding-year GLAD satellite observations provided frequency of water detection **conditional on pixels ever detected as water**, separate from the area fraction with detected water. This is not field-measured pond hydroperiod.

Strictly prior strong-calling history was reconstructed at the same physical sites. Two separate matched-pair analyses had different eligibility denominators: 4,040 source comparisons for earlier strong calling and 3,907 for conditional preceding-year satellite water-detection frequency. Environmental and survey covariates were matched within their documented source protocols. The whole-region suitability pattern and within-route differences were kept separate to avoid attributing geographically shared environment to direct species interactions.

### 2.4. Stage-structured models: illustration of feasible and infeasible mechanisms

The original main research mechanism comparison represented freshwater vegetation and three aquatic guilds as interacting components of a stage-structured seasonal predation system with delayed annual recruitment. From 3,600 unique mechanism configurations, an initial screen and a common evaluation of 20 candidates across 24 fixed nuisance-setting blocks tested whether conditional survival/hazard contrasts and rank-regression directions corresponding to the geographical analysis could be jointly produced. The reported finalist was a selected **model-internal example**, not a statistical fit to site-specific predator densities or observed acoustic CallingIndex. We retained the full failure set and the **positive empirical versus negative simulated three-way interaction** rather than only successful pairwise directions.

A subsequent and distinct model line (#146–#155) used the original 120-day within-season predation kernel and delayed annual recurrence, then imposed integer cohort sizes through binomial survival and stage completion and Poisson recruitment/immigration. It explicitly allowed annual movement among eight **synthetic ring patches**. Local reoccupation was a true integer N=0→N>0 transition, and three-year persistence and local recruitment were reported separately, with right-censoring. Synthetic mobility here means transferring a fixed annual fraction (0.15 in the principal comparisons) of surviving or recruited cohorts; it is not a field-estimated dispersal parameter or the individual stages of the separate #147 ABM.

The exploratory finite-count experiments compared original hypothetical biological-rate vectors, salamander growth levels, dragonfly aquatic exposure durations, stage-specific directed predator edges and arbitrary counts-per-density scales q=3,6,9,12. The high-local-recruitment contrasts used the original biological-rate ID12 with its rho parameter replaced by the ID0 value; with external immigration OFF, rho and conversion entered annual local recruitment **only through their product** and were not separately identifiable. The four active directed larval predation pathways were salamander→frog, odonate→frog, odonate→salamander and salamander→odonate. In these particular source timing regimes, completed frog→emerging odonate predation had no temporal overlap; a separate third stage-timing context from an earlier model experiment did permit that edge. We do not conflate their results.

Twelve independent random-seed labels per frozen synthetic treatment were used for the principal model comparisons. Habitat cases, source-factorial cells, repeated years and reoccupation events were **not** treated as independent biological or Monte Carlo replicates. Intermediate q=6 and q=9 tests and selected rate-axis replication followed earlier outcomes and are labelled post hoc. No process-model parameters were fitted to NAAMP choruses, the observed model-derived suitability surfaces or measured larval mortality in the monitored wetlands.

### 2.5. Contrasts and interpretation

Observed acoustic concentration, regional geographical associations, model hazard contrasts and long-run patch occupancy are intentionally **different estimands**. We compare the empirical and theoretical findings as a hierarchy of constraints, not as a single causal effect or an independent cross-validation. Statistical uncertainty intervals in the acoustic and macro analysis reflect their stated survey-level or route-level source procedures; finite-population seed-block intervals quantify only Monte Carlo variability conditional on synthetic inputs and are not confidence intervals for real ecological mechanisms.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Rainfall-related activation is spatially selective, not simply uniform

Across the 4,236 matched wetter–drier comparisons, wet-state acoustic increases frequently involved transitions from silence directly to overlapping or full chorus rather than only increased intensity at already calling sites. The spatial unit of change was broader than an isolated listening station, but it did not resemble uniform amplification across every route stop.

Among 2,916 comparisons with prior physical-site history, the observed within-taxon multi-site concentration statistic was **1.650**, compared with **1.353** from the principal source comparator, a residual of **0.297**. The simulated residual 95% range was **−0.132 to 0.119**, with source plus-one Monte Carlo **P=0.000999**. A held-out rainfall × historical-use comparison predicted concentration **1.332**. The excess lay especially in the deep multi-stop tail: CallingIndex 2–3 accounted for **97.3%** of third-and-later-stop spread. Strong chorusing in the wet run preferentially reappeared at historically stronger physical sites for the same taxon (prior-site coefficient **β=0.151**, 95% CI **0.129–0.173**).

These are observations of **where and when vocal activity was recorded**, not evidence that individual frogs moved between stations or that those sites produced more juveniles.

### 3.2. The geographical association with odonates depends on caudate context

In the separately filtered 4,248-site macroecological dataset, regional Spearman correlation between strong-calling frequency and modelled Odonata environmental suitability was weakly negative (**−0.095** with uniform-background SDMs; **−0.086** with target-group backgrounds). When locations were stratified by the suitability of pond-breeding Caudata, the uniform-background Odonata–calling correlation was **+0.101** in the low-Caudata stratum and **−0.284** in the high stratum; the high-minus-low difference was **−0.385** (95% route-bootstrap interval **−0.509 to −0.240**). For target-group models, the corresponding correlations were **−0.004** and **−0.183**, a difference of **−0.179** (**−0.328 to −0.029**). Thus the strength and in one specification the apparent direction of regional association varied with the third guild's modelled suitability.

Within-route associations were weaker and more sensitive to the statistical specification than whole-region relationships. The result identifies context dependence in **geographical association**; it does not demonstrate increased predation by odonate larvae at high-Caudata sites.

### 3.3. Vegetation, antecedent water and acoustic history add distinct constraints

The geographical rank regression of strong-calling frequency showed negative Odonata × Caudata and Odonata × vegetation coefficients under both background designs. The Caudata × vegetation estimate was positive in both but uncertain under target-group backgrounds. The full three-way Odonata × Caudata × vegetation term was **+0.037** (95% interval **+0.008 to +0.068**) in the uniform-background, 3-km whole-region model and **+0.027** (**−0.024 to +0.073**) in the target-group model. Its positive effect was therefore not robustly confirmed across all specifications or within-route contrasts. The 3-km landscape vegetation measure did not isolate a particular pond refuge mechanism.

The 4,040 strictly matched strong-versus-silent comparisons showed **27.68 percentage points** more previous strong-chorus site use at currently strong sites (95% route-component interval **25.07 to 30.17**). In the distinct 3,907 matched comparisons eligible for water-detected pixels, the preceding-year 3-km satellite water-detection frequency was **1.481 percentage points lower** on the stronger-calling side (interval **−2.601 to −0.294**). Detected water **area** did not show a comparably clear directional difference. These two results concern prior use and satellite appearance of water, not observed pond residence times or causal colonization.

### 3.4. The broad geographical interaction does not have a unique model explanation

In the first main-stage mechanism catalogue, one selected model candidate produced the three targeted pairwise rank-regression directions jointly with survival/hazard contrasts in **one of 24 common sensitivity settings**. The candidate required, in that selected configuration, caudate-to-frog protection by vegetation, odonate removal of caudates, and a vegetation-dependent density response in odonate predation. Conversely, direct caudate removal of odonates was unnecessary for this local success. Absolute frog survival was not forced close to zero in that selected case (minimum frog survival approximately **0.377**).

But the model's three-way regression term was **negative** (approximately **−0.048** on a coarse grid and **−0.010** on a finer grid) whereas the corresponding positive uniform-background empirical estimate was **+0.037**. Other rate settings did not recover a broad positive pattern. Since the observed predictors are suitability and landscape vegetation, and the model predictors are synthetic supply and refuge/functional-response terms, this failure is **a constraint on a candidate explanation, not a formal empirical model-rejection probability**. It is not appropriate to claim that the catalogue identified the cause of real calling patterns.

### 3.5. Demographic consequences of movement are conditional on direct predation, recruitment and population scale

The separate finite-N extensions demonstrated that reoccupation events and long-term persistence are not interchangeable endpoints. Under one selected synthetic high-local-recruitment regime and q=3, annual movement increased year-80 frog patch occupancy at low salamander growth but reduced it at fast growth. The active directed salamander-larva→frog-larva predation edge was necessary for that strict positive-to-negative movement effect in the selected model: removal abolished the reversal in both plant-input settings, while removal of each of the other three active directed edges individually did not. Retaining **only** salamander→frog predation was sufficient to reproduce the reversal in the same restricted synthetic high-recruitment regime. With all direct predation edges disabled, the growth-dependent reversal disappeared.

These intervention results were strongly conditional. With lower synthetic local recruitment the reversal was absent; among four original hypothetical process-rate vectors, a fast-growth sign switch occurred under only **one of four**. More decisively, when the arbitrary integer count scale increased, the strict source reversal occurred in **two of two fixed plant-input settings at q=3, one of two at q=6, and zero of two at q=9 or q=12**, under both the all-active and salamander→frog-only source networks. At larger q, year-80 occupied-patch values often approached the lower bound of **0/8** or upper bound of **8/8**, limiting the ability of movement to shift the binary occupancy count. The average growth-associated modification of the movement effect under salamander→frog-only was **−4.930, −1.063, +0.083 and +0.292 patches** across q=3,6,9,12.

The models thus provide a **demonstration of contingent demographic outcomes** under a defined trophic edge, life-stage growth, local recruitment and demographic stochasticity—not evidence that NAAMP choruses were generated by salamander predation or that nature has a critical population size corresponding to any of these q values.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Three levels of spatial organization, not one continuous observation

The empirical contribution is clearest when the two acoustic outcomes are kept distinct. At the scale of a favourable night, a species can enter strong chorus across multiple separated stations beyond expectations from its rainfall sensitivity and previous local site propensity. Over repeated survey years, strong calling is disproportionately associated with historically used locations. At a wider continental geographical scale, strong-call frequency co-varies with the environmental suitability of other freshwater guilds, and that association depends on the third guild and landscape context.

The shared NAAMP origin does not make these three estimates independent replications. Rather, they are compatible observations at different sampling grains. Their conjunction motivates the hypothesis that a temporally pulsed behavioural response is expressed on a nonrandom landscape template shaped by multiple ecological constraints. The source analyses cannot alone tell us whether those constraints operate through local habitat, demographic recruitment, acoustic detection, shared species distributions or predator effects.

### 4.2. Why a third guild matters, and why it remains an association

The contrast between high and low Caudata suitability is much more informative than the small pooled negative correlation with Odonata suitability alone. It shows that a single regional two-guild association cannot adequately represent the environmental contexts in which strong chorus is recorded. However, the within-route attenuation and sensitivity to the SDM background caution against a local species-interaction interpretation. A broadly suitable landscape for both guilds might covary with temperature, wetland type or geography even in the complete absence of local predation.

Likewise, landscape vegetation does not directly distinguish physical refuge for frogs from refuge for salamanders, emergence substrate for odonates or a change in predator functional response. The uneven confidence in the two-way versus three-way terms must remain visible. This is a reason to design new pond-level co-observations, not to relabel modelled spatial suitability as local predator abundance.

### 4.3. The value of model failure and conditional rescue

The stage-structured results do not validate a single mechanism for the observed acoustic and suitability associations. The original model catalogue's isolated success and opposite-sign three-way coefficient explicitly prevent that reading. Nevertheless, the synthetic simulations clarify how different biological interpretations can lead to very different long-run predictions. In some configurations, annual movement produces local reoccupation but no lasting population retention. In a selected finite-N regime, faster salamander growth and direct salamander→frog larval predation change a movement benefit into an occupancy loss. That same switch can disappear when the local-recruitment strength, process-rate background or population count scale changes.

The most defensible theoretical contribution is therefore not a universal sign law but a **boundary on mechanistic explanation**: direct predator presence, historical occupancy and even abundant recolonization events do not uniquely specify whether dispersal will maintain a local frog population. A mathematical caveat reinforces this: in the external-immigration-OFF source recurrence, rho and the conversion parameter contribute as a product to local recruitment and cannot be claimed to identify two independent mechanisms.

The q sensitivity was an exploratory expansion after the q=12 result; it is not a field-calibrated population-size threshold. Further, in the salamander→frog-only model, a nominal vegetation function protecting salamanders from odonates loses its intended mechanism when the odonate→salamander edge is disabled. Two retained plant parameter settings are therefore not evidence for two independently active protective mechanisms. These explicit limits are important even within a single integrated paper.

### 4.4. An actionable observation-to-process test

A decisive next study would repeatedly measure, within the **same identified physical ponds and reproductive seasons**, adult calling effort and detectability, aquatic vegetation/refuge configuration, frog and salamander larval densities and sizes, odonate larval presence, hydroperiod and recruitment over subsequent years. This is the observation-to-process bridge absent from the present sources. It would allow competing explanations—shared environment, direct salamander→frog predation, odonate-mediated indirect effects and vegetation-specific density responses—to be evaluated on a common unit and temporally appropriate scale. Site-specific historical calling propensity and the original rainfall pulse statistic would provide independent prospective acoustic predictions if the required field components were actually observed.

Until then, inferences about actual occupancy rescue, realized trophic edges, individual coordination and breeding success remain hypotheses. It would be especially inappropriate to treat calling in one season as the consequence of larvae that have not yet matured in that season.

### 4.5. Scope, generality and data provenance

Our evidence spans a large repeated acoustic programme and independently constructed environmental suitability and water-detection products, but not an independent field validation of trophic processes. Across the observational analyses, background-model choices and geographical pairing determine the level of inference. Within the numerical analyses, twelve random seeds per fixed treatment do not make hypothetical parameter draws or model scenarios into independent ecological replicates. Stage arrival timing matters: frog-to-emerging-odonate reverse predation is exactly inactive in the two developmental contexts used for the later finite-N experiment, although active in a distinct third context of the earlier source model. This particular absence must not be generalized to all real amphibian–odonate interactions.

The resulting contribution is one **evidence-calibrated integrative study**: spatial selectivity of frog chorusing is observed, multi-guild environmental context is associated with the sites where it recurs, and process models delineate which demographic interpretations are possible or fragile. The paper does not infer that the simulated dynamics caused the geographical or rainfall-associated acoustic results. These three analytical components share a programme and hypotheses rather than a calibrated likelihood linking observed calling to modelled larval survival.

## 5. Conclusion

Repeated North American surveys reveal non-uniform within-taxon allocation of rainfall-associated frog choruses, persistent use of particular physical calling sites, and geographical calling associations that vary with other freshwater guilds and vegetation. Stage-structured predator and dispersal models show how direct larval predation, recruitment and finite population size can generate different long-term outcomes, but their dependence on narrow settings and failure to reproduce one empirical three-way term prohibit a unique field-mechanism claim. A fuller understanding of frog breeding-site spatial structure requires retaining both the **observed behavioural pattern** and the **limits of its demographic interpretation**.
