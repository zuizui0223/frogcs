# Third-guild context reshapes frog calling associations across North America: testing trophic explanations with stage-structured models

## Abstract

Ecological associations are often summarized in pairs, although their apparent strength may depend on other members of the community and habitat context. We examined this possibility using 4,248 repeatedly surveyed frog acoustic locations across North America, occurrence-based environmental suitability for 28 frog, caudate and odonate taxa, and stage-structured trophic models. The association between odonate suitability and frequent strong frog calling was weak overall but differed markedly between low- and high-caudate-suitability locations: high-minus-low rank-correlation contrasts were −0.385 and −0.179 under two distribution-model backgrounds. Vegetation also modified pairwise associations, whereas the positive three-way term was sensitive to background and spatial specification. A site-mapped pattern-oriented comparison found no candidate among 132 fixed trophic scenarios that jointly matched six observed statistics; separate model-native ABC simulations could reproduce idealized interaction signs but did not establish an empirical field fit. Finite-population extensions further showed that the demographic consequences of the same larval predation assumptions depend on recruitment and stochastic population scale. Thus third-guild context is a reproducible feature of broad-scale geographical association, while the processes capable of producing it remain constrained, rather than identified, by mechanistic modelling.

**Keywords:** macroecology; freshwater community; acoustic breeding activity; third-guild context; pattern-oriented modelling; approximate Bayesian computation; stage-structured food web; demographic stochasticity

## Introduction

Interactions among species are often interpreted through pairwise associations. Yet a pairwise relationship can change across places containing different other species, habitats or life-history conditions. A negative association between a potential predator and prey may reflect direct predation, indirect release by a third guild, seasonal habitat overlap, shared environmental filtering or uneven observation. Distinguishing these possibilities matters if associations are to inform explanations of community organization rather than simply catalogue patterns of geographical co-occurrence.

Freshwater breeding habitats offer a useful system because several biological roles can intersect. Frog larvae can be consumed by aquatic salamanders and odonate nymphs; these predators may in turn interact with each other, depending on body size and developmental stage. Aquatic vegetation can modify direct access to prey, protect different guilds and alter density-dependent feeding responses. These mechanisms make a simple prediction tempting: the geographical association between frog breeding activity and odonates could differ in places environmentally suitable for caudates, and the role of vegetation might be contingent on which trophic pathway is operating. Yet the same pattern could emerge from shared climate, wetland type, geographic structure or differences in what is detected.

Frog acoustic surveys add a distinctive but indirect observational axis. Male calling is associated with breeding activity and can be measured repeatedly across large regions, but acoustic indices do not measure larval abundance or reproductive success. Earlier exploratory analysis of the North American Amphibian Monitoring Program (NAAMP) found rainfall-linked chorusing concentrated at recurrent physical sites; this motivated an examination of which community and landscape contexts characterize strongly calling locations. That previous result is not an independent validation of the present study, which draws on the same monitoring programme.

Our first question is **whether a third guild alters the geographical association between frog calling and potential odonate habitat suitability**. We ask whether the association differs along the potential distribution of pond-breeding caudates and varies with landscape vegetation, under multiple distribution-model backgrounds and spatial controls. Second, we ask **which stage-structured trophic pathways can generate corresponding conditional patterns and how robust those pathways are to biological-rate choices**. Third, we test **whether a model-based prediction about dispersal remains stable when recruitment and finite population size are changed**. The third question addresses the transferability of a candidate demographic explanation, not a claim that simulated larval predation caused observed adult calls.

The study brings geographical evidence and mechanistic constraints into one research paper while maintaining their different units of inference. Its possible general contribution is **a way to discriminate context-dependent ecological patterns from overly flexible trophic explanations**, rather than an assertion that environmental suitability establishes actual predation.

## Materials and methods

### Continental acoustic and guild-environment dataset

We used the source-verified NAAMP subset with **4,248 repeatedly surveyed acoustic listening locations** (at least six surveys across three years) and 432 route components in its original broad-scale analysis. The response was the proportion of surveys in which at least one focal frog taxon reached CallingIndex ≥2. This is a site-level frequency of strong recorded calling, not occupancy, density or egg-to-adult recruitment. Physical listening sites are not automatically independent ponds; connected route components were considered in resampling and fixed comparisons.

Potential freshwater guilds were assembled from original species checklists, GBIF occurrence records and interaction evidence in GloBI. Of 30 interaction-supported candidates, **28 taxa** met the distribution-modelling criteria: **15 frogs, seven caudates and six odonates**. Occurrence-derived environmental suitability was projected to monitoring sites under both **uniform-background and target-group-background** model specifications. The pooled guild measures summarize relative niche suitability within the retained modelled species, not actual wetland predator numbers, the entire local species assemblage or observed species interactions at those survey locations.

### Third-guild conditioning and landscape vegetation

We quantified the regional association between strong-calling frequency and modelled odonate suitability and compared it across **low versus high pond-breeding-caudate suitability**. Differences in Spearman rank correlation were assessed by source-prescribed route-level resampling. We separately examined a rank-standardized model with odonate, caudate and landscape-vegetation main effects, all pairwise terms and the three-way term.

Vegetation was a source EarthEnv wetland-vegetation landscape indicator aggregated within **3 km** of monitoring locations from approximately **1-km** pixels, with 1-km-neighbourhood sensitivity. It is not a field measurement of submerged stems, protected microhabitat or emergence surface. Results from the full region were kept distinct from associations measured **within routes**, where shared geography and environmental suitability are partly constrained. Because both SDM backgrounds and several spatial resolutions reuse the same source occurrences, their agreement is a sensitivity result rather than independent external replication.

### Water history and prior use: supplementary observational context

Within source-matched strong-versus-silent site comparisons, we retained **4,040 pairs** for strictly prior strong-calling history and **3,907 pairs** with defined preceding-year satellite water-detection frequency. The latter is conditional on pixels where water had been detected and is separate from the fraction of nearby area ever detected as water; it is **not** a direct measurement of pond hydroperiod. These are secondary context measurements with different eligibility denominators. We do not use them to infer realized predator pathways or a demographic recruitment process.

### Trophic and demographic explanation tests

We first evaluated the existing full main source catalogue of **3,600 unique trophic structures**, derived after collapsing redundant logical combinations. The screen combined larval frog, salamander and odonate predation, life-stage timing and size sensitivity, vegetation-mediated protection, food-web indirect effects and alternative odonate functional responses. The candidate subset was evaluated under common variations in arrival timing, aquatic duration, feeding and recruitment; **20 configurations × 24 shared sensitivity contexts** underwent source-verified comparison. A successful empirical-style *directional* screen required negative odonate×caudate and odonate×vegetation rank coefficients and a positive caudate×vegetation term alongside previously specified survival/hazard constraints. We also assessed the sign of the observed three-way term, which was **not** reproduced by the selected candidate. Model supply parameters were never equated with species-distribution suitability or calling frequency at real ponds.

In a distinct extension, the original **120-day seasonal predation kernel** and delayed annual cohort recurrence were coupled with integer binomial/Poisson cohort sampling, eight synthetic ring patches and annual movement of surviving and/or newly recruited cohorts over **80 years**. Synthetic recruitment intensity, size-related larval predation and direct predator-edge deletion were manipulated. An illustrative higher-local-recruitment regime used original hypothetical rate-draw ID12 with its rho parameter set to the ID0 value. Under immigration OFF, rho and the cohort recruitment conversion parameter occur through their **product**, and therefore do not identify two independent processes. The source's frog-completed→emerging-odonate edge was temporally inactive in the particular two timing contexts of this series; an earlier distinct timing context did permit it.

The key model-native endpoint was the **difference in year-80 occupied frog patches (movement ON minus movement OFF)**, not acoustic calling, and a prespecified strict *S-growth-dependent sign reversal* required a positive movement effect in both lower-growth contexts and a negative effect in both higher-growth contexts. We compared networks with all direct larval predation edges, only salamander→frog predation, salamander→frog disabled and all direct predation disabled; synthetic integer count-per-density scales **q=3,6,9,12**; and **12 independent random-seed labels** per frozen setting. All units and null controls were kept distinct. The q6/q9 grid followed the q12 loss of reversal and is explicitly **post hoc exploratory**.

### Evidence and inference limits

The acoustic data, guild suitability correlations, main model screen and later finite-N simulations yield different estimands. The simulations are not fitted to the acoustic survey, and repeated habitat cases or simulation years are not independent wild observations. Consequently, agreement in a model-native sign is a **feasibility result**, not a causal estimate of larval predation in the NAAMP wetlands. Confidence intervals from observational route resampling and finite-N random-seed Monte Carlo intervals must not be conflated.

## Results

### Third-guild suitability changes a weak regional two-guild association

Across 4,248 acoustic sites, regional Spearman association between odonate suitability and strong frog calling was **−0.095** under the uniform-background model and **−0.086** under the target-group background. The pooled coefficient alone is therefore small. However, conditional on pond-breeding-caudate suitability, the relationship varied substantially. In the uniform-background analysis, the odonate–calling correlation was **+0.101** in low-caudate-suitability locations and **−0.284** in high-caudate-suitability locations. The high-minus-low contrast was **−0.385** (95% route-bootstrap interval **−0.509 to −0.240**). Under target-group backgrounds the corresponding correlations were **−0.004** and **−0.183**, yielding a contrast **−0.179** (**−0.328 to −0.029**).

Thus the **contrast in association strength** persists across the two background specifications, although an apparent *change from positive to negative correlation* does not: the low-caudate group is approximately uncorrelated under the target-group model. Within-route and more tightly conditioned comparisons were less stable than the whole-region contrasts. The geographic pattern is clear as a source-specific conditional association but remains compatible with spatially structured shared environments.

### Landscape and water contexts add conditionality, not a confirmed field refuge mechanism

The main 3-km landscape models gave odonate×caudate standardized rank interaction estimates **−0.166** (95% interval −0.229 to −0.108) for uniform background and **−0.086** (−0.151 to −0.023) for target-group background. Odonate×vegetation coefficients were **−0.087** (−0.124 to −0.048) and **−0.064** (−0.115 to −0.014). Caudate×vegetation point estimates were **+0.058** (+0.015 to +0.101) and **+0.046** (−0.022 to +0.109), respectively.

The three-way odonate×caudate×vegetation estimate was **+0.037** (+0.008 to +0.068) under uniform backgrounds but **+0.027** (−0.024 to +0.073) under target-group backgrounds, with spatial sensitivity. Accordingly, the positive three-way term **cannot be treated as a robustly established tri-trophic field interaction**. In separate fixed site comparisons, strong-calling locations showed **+27.68 percentage points** (95% interval +25.07 to +30.17) greater prior strong-calling history across 4,040 pairs, whereas preceding-year *conditional water-detection frequency* was **−1.481 percentage points** (−2.601 to −0.294) across 3,907 eligible pairs. Those estimates concern different observations and cannot be combined into a causal food-web coefficient.

### Integrated ABC–POM: conditional process compatibility does not yet identify an empirical demographic mechanism

We combined two existing pattern-oriented model assessments and source-matched demographic experiments in an explicit **sequential hypothesis-assessment framework**. A first, site-mapped POM comparison used the *same 4,248 monitoring locations* and a shared global-rank definition of six statistics: the O×S, O×vegetation, S×vegetation and O×S×vegetation regression terms, and odonate–calling correlations in low- and high-Caudata-suitability subsets. These were compared with archived model-generated F_B surfaces after a fixed O/S-suitability-to-supply rank mapping, with spatial-block covariance from 56 geographic blocks and 999 resamples. This comparison was *not* a field-calibrated larval-to-adult calling model.

Among **132 fixed-parameter source scenarios (104 distinct response surfaces)**, **none** jointly reproduced all six empirical statistics within their respective descriptive 95% spatial-block intervals under either distribution-model background. The closest uniform-background scenario (case_005, numerically identical to case_009) had distance **10.827** and **zero of six** marginal pattern intervals covered; the closest target-group candidate (case_036) had distance **8.262** and **two of six**. In particular, the uniform-background source site-POM low-Caudata correlation was **+0.032** under its *global-rank* definition, whereas candidate case_005 generated **−0.717**. This is not identical to the separately reported stratum-specific-rank correlation **+0.101**; the two estimands must not be substituted for each other. The previously assessed directional catalogue also exposed a reproducible numerical conflict between negative odonate×vegetation and more negative odonate–calling association in the high-Caudata stratum. The absence of a six-pattern match is an adequacy failure **within the tested fixed library**, not proof that a three-guild trophic explanation is impossible in nature.

A separate model-native POM/ABC library tested whether original stage-structured trophic dynamics could produce the **four directions OS−, OV−, SV+, OSV+** on a *balanced synthetic O×S×vegetation grid*. The original 120-day seasonal hazard kernel and 80-year delayed cohort recurrence were genuinely simulated across **12 process structures ×256 hypothetical biological rate vectors ×two paired reverse-predation flags**. One original-stage/baseline-handling/no-risk-refuge class generated all four model-native signs under **65 of 256 independent hypothetical rate vectors** (25.39%). However, this model-derived F_B contrast is **not** the observed six-pattern 4,248-site fit, and those rates are not empirical posteriors. Subsequent enumeration of **108 canonical plant/stage model classes** found that preferred plant roles changed under alternative structural prior weightings, precluding a unique selected vegetation pathway. In the original main screen of 3,600 trophic structures, the best restricted candidate similarly matched selected pairwise directions and hazard/survival conditions in only **one of 24** common sensitivity settings and predicted a **negative** O×S×vegetation term (**−0.0482** or **−0.0104** with different input grids) rather than the positive uniform-background empirical point estimate (**+0.037**).

Finally, source-preserving finite-population simulations asked what long-term consequences **hypothetical** trophic networks can generate once annual recruitment, stage-specific larval predation and migration are present. In one selected high-local-recruitment source regime and arbitrary count-per-density scale **q=3**, the direct salamander-larva→frog-larva predation edge was conditionally necessary and sufficient for a strict low-S-growth positive/high-S-growth negative **movement effect on year-80 occupied frog patches**. The same sign-reversal criterion was met in **two of two fixed plant-input settings at q=3, one at q=6, and none at q=9 or q=12** when S→F predation was active. The corresponding mean S-growth modifier under S→F-only was **−4.930, −1.063, +0.083 and +0.292 occupied patches** across those four synthetic count scales. A comparable growth-induced reversal appeared in only **one of four** original hypothetical rate vectors. The direct S→F edge is therefore neither empirically identified nor sufficient to produce that outcome across the tested demographic settings.

**These are not yet three outputs from a single ABC posterior.** The archived 132 *site-mapped* fixed models, model-native 12/108-class biological-rate libraries and selected finite-N demographic experiments have **different candidate/parameter and observation definitions**. A common model×θ library has not been source-matched across all layers. Consequently **no field-conditioned posterior over mechanisms and no posterior-predictive distribution of long-run frog occupancy have been estimated**. The completed evidence jointly establishes a stricter result: apparently compatible model-native interaction signs do not imply adequacy to the observed multi-pattern geography, and the biological implications of an illustrative compatible model can themselves vary with recruitment and demographic stochasticity.

## Discussion

### Third-party context is a measurable feature of macroecological association

Our primary empirical result is not that odonates suppress frog breeding, but that a single pooled odonate–calling correlation conceals strong variation across the potential caudate guild. That conditional contrast is visible in two differently specified distribution models. It supplies a rationale for incorporating third-guild context into broad-scale analyses of ecological relationships instead of treating every pairwise association as uniform across environments.

However, two properties limit its interpretation. First, the predictors are modelled **environmental suitability**, not co-observed larval predation or even pond-scale species abundance. Second, the relationship is strongest at the regional scale and becomes more uncertain within routes. A spatially varying shared habitat template remains a competing explanation, rather than a nuisance automatically excluded by an interaction coefficient. An upper-tier ecological claim would need to show which part of the contrast remains after explicit geographic and environmental decomposition.

### ABC–POM as a single mechanism test, with distinct adequacy and prediction layers

The most useful implication of the integrated model analysis is the contrast between **generating idealized interaction signs** and **matching jointly measured geographic patterns**. The model-native four-direction ABC demonstrates that hypothetical developmental, refuge and predation parameters can jointly produce the required signs, whereas the mapped-site POM shows that the available fixed scenario library does not yet reproduce the six observational statistics. The full stage-structured catalogue's inability to reproduce the observed three-way term reinforces this caution. The fitted effects involve odonate and caudate **environmental suitability**, while the process outputs involve aquatic F cohorts and synthetic predator supply; similar mathematical interaction terms do not turn these into equivalent empirical variables.

The finite-population results then address **conditional consequence** of a candidate trophic assumption: movement may generate more local reoccupation events without maintaining more occupied patches, and even a model-native necessary/sufficient predator edge may cease to generate the same outcome under different count scales or rate vectors. These are conditional counterfactuals, not an additional observed likelihood. Treating demographic occupancy as a second ABC acceptance target would be circular because the source surveys provide no corresponding long-term F-cohort occupancy measurements.

A genuine one-model ABC–POM extension would require the *same* model structure, rate vector, vegetation and stage parameters to produce site-level pattern statistics through an independently defensible observation process and, without changing those values, generate long-run occupancy predictions. Model rejection should occur at the site-pattern adequacy stage, before interpreting class weights or population forecasts. If all candidate site models are inadequate, the appropriate result is **no empirically conditioned mechanistic prediction**, not selecting the least-bad source as biologically true.

### What could elevate a geographic pattern to a broad ecological principle?

The combined findings point to a testable programme rather than to a finished causal bridge. A discriminating field test would collect frog calling, larval frog and salamander sizes and counts, odonate nymph counts, aquatic vegetation and hydroperiod **in the same physical ponds and biologically appropriate seasons**, followed by subsequent recruitment. A formal observation model could then link process-model states to adult calling probability, survey effort and weather. In addition, the geographical caudate-conditioned odonate–calling contrast should be evaluated against predeclared shared climate, wetland and spatial autocorrelation controls in genuinely held-out regional units.

The general insight supported now is modest but useful: **ecological relationships inferred at one scale need not retain the same apparent strength or demographic meaning when third-guild context and life-history processes are considered**. The next evidentiary threshold is to distinguish third-guild-mediated interactions from coincident suitability and to test mechanistic predictions on commensurate data. Without that threshold, a broad conceptual mechanism should remain a falsifiable hypothesis rather than a demonstrated explanation.

## Conclusion

Across repeatedly monitored North American freshwater sites, the geography of frog strong calling is associated with odonate environmental suitability in ways that differ across potential caudate guild and vegetation context. Stage-structured model tests show that proposed trophic and demographic explanations can be highly conditional, and in important respects fail to reproduce the empirical pattern. **Third-guild context is observed in the macroecological association; the specific causal trophic explanation remains open.**
